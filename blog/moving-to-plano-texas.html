Moving to Plano, Texas: An Honest Family Guide

Kristen Carpentier Licensed TX Realtor®  ·  July 16, 2026  ·  11 min read

So you are thinking about moving to Plano, Texas, and there is a decent chance you did not exactly pick Plano. Plano picked you. If your company just moved you here, or you are staring down an offer at one of the big campuses, this is the honest rundown of what it is actually like to raise a family in Plano, so you can decide whether it fits, and which part of it fits you. No fluff, no pressure, just real numbers.

I am Kristen Carpentier, a licensed Texas Realtor here in Dallas Fort Worth and a mom of four. We raised our family right here, and I spend most of my weeks helping families land in the right DFW suburb. Plano is one of the largest corporate employment centers in North Texas, so a lot of the families I talk to end up here because a job did the choosing. Let me walk you through the whole picture.

Quick answer: A lot of families move to Plano, Texas for a job, since Toyota, JPMorgan Chase, Frito-Lay and others cluster around Legacy West. You land in an established, built-out suburb where most addresses feed into a strong Plano ISD (rated A by Niche), though a small pocket on the north side is zoned to Frisco ISD. Plano ISD also splits high school into a 9th to 10th grade campus and a separate 11th to 12th senior high, so verify the district and feeder for your exact address before you buy.

Why Families Land in Plano: The Jobs

Here is what makes Plano different from almost every other suburb I talk about. A lot of families do not choose Plano for the schools or the houses first. They choose it because their paycheck moved here.

Plano is one of the biggest corporate hubs in North Texas. Toyota's North American headquarters is here. JPMorgan Chase has a massive campus. Frito-Lay, FedEx Office, Liberty Mutual, and Capital One are all right here too, a lot of them clustered around an area called Legacy West. So if one of you got hired or transferred into one of those, Plano is not really a maybe. It is the gravity. If you are weighing the offer itself, my rundown of the top employers in the Dallas Texas area gives you a sense of who else is hiring nearby.

Here is a piece people miss: because there are so many big employers packed in here and just next door, it is often easier for the second spouse to find work too. When you are a two career family, that job density is a real practical advantage, not just a nice-to-have.

And Legacy West itself is not just offices. It is a whole walkable district of restaurants, shops, and a kind of downtown-at-work feel, so a lot of younger families and folks without kids yet actually live right there in the townhomes and apartments and walk to dinner. We will come back to that when we talk housing.

Moving to Plano: The House, Established vs New

Now let us talk about what your money buys, because this is where Plano feels different from the newer suburbs up north. Plano is grown up. It is built out. It is established.

Let me give you the rough tiers, and these move around, so verify when you are actually shopping. If you want that walk-to-dinner Legacy West lifestyle, townhomes and condos tend to start somewhere in the 300s. A single family home across Plano runs, very roughly, in the 500s on average, currently a median around $531K. But that average is doing a lot of work, because West Plano, closer to the corporate campuses, climbs well past that, while central and east Plano can give you more room in your budget.

Here is the honest tradeoff, and I want you to really hear it. When you buy in Plano, you are buying a neighborhood where the trees are already tall and the schools have been well regarded for years. You are not planting saplings and waiting for the place to grow up around you.

The flip side? A lot of Plano's homes were built a while ago, so you may be trading brand-new finishes for mature bones and a great location, and budgeting for some updates over time. If a shiny, never-lived-in master-planned house on a big lot is the dream, Plano will feel more established and more resale than new, and you will pay up for the newer pockets that do exist. Neither one is better. They are just different stages of life for a suburb.

One practical note if you buy an established Plano home: budget your inspection around the bones, not the finishes. On homes built in the 1980s, 1990s, or early 2000s, that means paying attention to roof and HVAC age, the foundation and drainage, the plumbing and sewer lines, and the electrical panel. Older does not mean problems. It means you plan for the remaining life of the big systems instead of being surprised by them.

One quick money note

I am not going to re-teach Texas property taxes here, I break the whole tax picture down in my DFW Family Relocation Guide. But the Plano-specific version is this: because Plano is mostly established, you run into fewer of those newer special tax districts (MUD or PID) than you do in the fast-growth suburbs further north. That is a pattern, not a guarantee, so always verify the full tax rate, any PID or special assessment, and the HOA dues on the exact home before you make an offer.

Schools: The Plano ISD Split

Now, schools, and here is the wrinkle I promised. Plano Independent School District is large, established, and very well regarded, earning an A overall from Niche with an A+ for college prep. One address-level catch first: most of Plano feeds into Plano ISD, but a small pocket on the north side is actually zoned to Frisco ISD, so the city name alone does not tell you the district. Verify it for the exact home.

And then there is the structural piece: Plano ISD does something most DFW districts do not. It splits your high schoolers up.

In a lot of Plano, your kids go to one campus for ninth and tenth grade, then move to a separate senior high school for eleventh and twelfth. It is not good or bad, families have strong feelings in both directions, but you need to know it going in, because it shapes the feeder pattern your address lands in.

There is an upside to a big, established district like this. It is not growing so fast that they are constantly redrawing boundaries, which is a real thing families deal with in the fast-growth suburbs. Because Plano is more built-out, those boundaries tend to feel more settled, but always verify the current boundary for the exact address. And the district runs academies and choice programs that can be a great fit for a specific kind of student.

Here is the bigger point. As a Realtor, my job is not to tell you which schools are best, it is to make sure you know exactly which schools an address feeds into, ninth grade all the way through senior high. Go pull up the specific campuses on GreatSchools and on Niche, read how real families describe them, and match them to your own kid: the athlete, the one who needs a strong special education program, the one who is going to live in the band hall. Then confirm the boundary at the exact address before you sign anything, because in a district that splits, the assumption trap is very real. Location and Commute: Plano's Quiet Win

Now, location, because this is where Plano quietly wins. Plano sits right in the middle of everything. You have the Dallas North Tollway and US 75 running straight through, the Telecom Corridor jobs just south in Richardson, the Legacy West campuses right here, DART light rail with stations in Plano if you want a train option, and a straight shot down into Dallas, or out to either airport, when you need it.

So if your office is in Plano or anywhere along those corridors, your commute can be genuinely short, and that is the thing families underestimate until they have lived both versions. A fifteen minute drive to work gives you your evenings back. For a two-job family especially, that central position is gold, because you are not optimizing one commute and wrecking the other.

That said, test your actual commute, at your actual hour, before you settle on a part of town. A job right in Plano can put you fifteen minutes from your desk, but the citywide median commute is closer to twenty-nine minutes, and the Tollway at five o'clock is its own animal.

Family Lifestyle: It Is Already Built

Now, what is there to actually do with your family here, because a place you are moving to has to work on the weekends, not just Monday to Friday. This is one of the upsides of an established suburb. You are not waiting for the amenities to get built. They are already here.

Plano has real nature, not just retail: a couple of big nature preserves with miles of trails where you can completely forget you are in a city of nearly three hundred thousand people. There is the dining and shopping at Legacy West and the Shops at Legacy, a historic downtown Plano with local restaurants and weekend events, and the youth sports, the rec centers, and the libraries, the whole established infrastructure of a city that has been raising families for a long time. Most of the good stuff is right here in Plano, not a long drive away. For a lot of relocating families, that everything-is-already-here feeling is the thing that makes Plano finally feel like home.

Who Plano Is Not For

Let me be straight with you about who Plano is not for, because I would rather you hear it from me now. Plano is a built-out, busy, mature suburb.

If you are dreaming of acreage, quiet, and a small-town main street, that is a different suburb, that is more of a McKinney or an Argyle conversation. If you want the newest construction and the biggest new-build lots, you will be happier looking further north, and you can start with the new construction communities across DFW. A lot of Plano's housing is older, so if you do not want to update a kitchen or a bath, you will be shopping in the pricier, already-renovated end. And because Plano is established and central, the main corridors get busy, and the most sought-after pockets are priced like it.

But if what you want is to live close to a great job, in an established community, with a strong district and a genuinely central location, Plano is really hard to beat for that.

Where Families Look in Plano

So where do families actually look once they land on Plano? I am not going to rank neighborhoods for you, that is its own conversation, but the lay of the land is roughly this.

West Plano tends to draw families who want to be near the corporate campuses, and they pay a premium for it. Central and east Plano can open up more value and some of that older, tree-lined, big-yard charm. And the Legacy West area pulls the live-where-you-work, walk-to-dinner crowd. Which side fits you comes down to where you work, your budget, and the exact schools you research for each address.

If Plano is on your shortlist, I would usually have you weigh it against Frisco, Allen, and sometimes McKinney, depending on whether your bigger priority is corporate proximity, newer construction, or more room in the budget. One Tip If Your Company Is Relocating You

One honest tip if your company is relocating you, because corporate moves come with a clock. If you can, get out here for a couple of days before you commit. Drive the real commute, walk into a couple of the schools, and see the difference between West Plano and the rest with your own eyes.

And if the timeline is too tight to be sure, there is zero shame in renting for a year first. I would so much rather you rent and get the part of town right than rush a purchase to beat a relocation deadline and wish you had waited.

Before you commit to a part of Plano
  • Drive your actual commute, at your actual work hour, not midday
  • Confirm the exact Plano ISD feeder chain for the address, ninth grade through senior high
  • Compare West Plano against central and east Plano in person
  • Budget for updates if you buy an older, established home
  • Verify the current price and any tax district on the specific home
  • If the clock is tight, consider renting for a year before you buy
