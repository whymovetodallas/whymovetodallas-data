Is Moving from California to Dallas Worth It? (The Honest Answer)

Kristen Carpentier Licensed TX Realtor®  ·  June 9, 2026  ·  8 min read

I get this question almost every week, usually over the phone, usually from a parent who's already half-packed in their head: "Be honest with me — is moving from California to Dallas actually worth it?" And I'll give you the same answer I give them. For most families, yes — but only if you go in with your eyes open about what you're trading. The people who end up happy here did their homework. The people who end up frustrated skipped it.

So let's do the homework together. I'm not here to sell you on Texas — I'm a mom of four who raised my kids in these suburbs, and I'd rather you make the right call than just a call. Here's the honest math, the real tradeoffs, and where California families actually end up.

Quick answer: For families, moving from California to the Dallas suburbs is usually worth it — no state income tax means roughly $7,100 more take-home on a $100K salary, and your housing dollar stretches more than twice as far. The catch: you trade mild weather, the coast, and walkability for heat, more driving, and higher property tax rates.

The Honest Tradeoffs First

If a Realtor only tells you the good parts, find a different Realtor. Here's what you're genuinely giving up when you leave California, and I'd rather you hear it from me now than discover it in August.

The heat is real. June through September, North Texas runs hot — long stretches in the high 90s and triple digits with humidity on top. The A/C runs almost non-stop, and a 2,500–3,500 sq ft home commonly runs $250–$400 a month in summer versus $80–$150 in winter. The first summer is a shock if you're coming from the coast.

You will drive more. DFW is built around the car. Outside a few pockets, this is a get-in-the-SUV lifestyle, not a walk-to-the-coffee-shop one. Many northern-suburb commutes lean on toll roads, and regular commuters budget $80–$160 a month just in tolls. If walkability is non-negotiable, sit with that.

Property tax rates are higher. California's Proposition 13 starts from a 1% base rate plus voter-approved local bonds. In DFW, effective rates more commonly land in the 1.8%–2.2% range, and newer master-planned communities can carry MUD or PID surcharges on top. There's no state income tax to offset it — the whole tradeoff, which I break down below.

No mountains, no ocean. North Texas is prairie — lakes, not coastline; rolling, not dramatic. If weekend hikes in the Sierras or mornings at the beach are core to who your family is, that's a genuine loss to weigh, not a detail to gloss over.

Kristen's Take

None of these are dealbreakers for most families — but they're real. The families who thrive here decided the schools, the space, and the lower cost of living were worth the heat and the driving. The ones who struggled assumed Dallas would feel like a cheaper California. It doesn't. It's its own place.

The Real Cost-of-Living Math

Here's where the move starts to make sense for a lot of families. Two numbers do most of the work: what you keep from your paycheck, and what you pay for a house.

No state income tax. Texas doesn't have one. California, by contrast, has a steep income tax — a 12.3% top bracket, plus a 1% surcharge on taxable income over $1 million — so even a typical professional household pays a meaningful share every year. In one 2025 CNBC comparison, a $100,000 salary translated to about $7,100 more in annual take-home pay in Texas than in California — roughly $590 a month — purely because Texas has no state income tax (CNBC, 2025). Scale that to a $150K or $200K household and the gap grows fast.

A home that would cost $1.3M–$1.6M in much of coastal California has a DFW-suburb equivalent for well under half that. Property Tax vs No Income Tax — The Trade Californians Misjudge

This is the one that trips people up, so let's be precise about it. Texas property tax rates are higher than California's. That's true. But the comparison isn't apples to apples, because Texas gives back in two ways California doesn't.

First, there's no state income tax — so the higher property bill is offset by everything you're not paying on every paycheck all year. Second, the Texas homestead exemption removes $140,000 from the school-district portion of your home's appraised value (Texas Tax Code §11.13(b)). On a $500,000 home, that drops your school-taxable value to $360,000 before the rate is even applied.

Budget for that the same way a Californian budgets for earthquake or wildfire coverage — it's just a different risk.

About That "California Regret" — Who Actually Regrets It

You've probably seen the headlines: Californians who moved to Texas and wish they hadn't. They're real, and I take them seriously. But when you read past the headline, a pattern shows up — and it's almost never "Texas was bad." It's an expectations mismatch.

The people who tend to regret it expected Dallas to be a cheaper version of the life they had: walkable, mild, with the coast an hour away. They often landed in the urban core chasing that feeling, then ran into the heat, the driving, and a city that works differently than San Francisco or LA — without the suburb upsides of schools, a yard, and real value.

The families who are happy did the opposite. They came for what DFW is actually great at — room to raise kids, strong districts, and a dollar that goes further — and picked a suburb that fit their commute and budget, not the one that looked most like home. That single decision, researching the suburb fit instead of assuming, separates the two camps more often than anything else.

Most families who move here for the schools and the space are much happier with the tradeoffs. The regret almost always comes from expecting Texas to feel like California for less money. It won't — and the families who accept that early are the ones who fall in love with it.

One honest note: I won't rank suburbs by "safety" or tell you which areas to avoid — that's not something a Realtor can ethically characterize, and the honest answer is that it's your call to research. For crime and safety data, look at independent sources like NeighborhoodScout or City-Data and draw your own conclusions before you buy.

Who This Move Isn't Right For

Honesty cuts both ways, so let me say plainly who I'd tell to slow down before making a California-to-Dallas move. There's no shame in any of these — they're just signs the fit may not be there.

This move may not fit if…
  • You need true, everyday walkability — DFW is car-first almost everywhere
  • Coastal weather and mild summers are non-negotiable for your family
  • You're tied to a daily commute deep into the urban core, not a suburb
  • You're expecting Texas to feel like California for less money

None of these mean DFW is the wrong place — plenty of people make it work anyway. They just mean the move won't be the easy win it tends to be for a family chasing schools, space, and a lower cost of living. If two or more of those describe you, it's worth a longer conversation before you commit.

So — Is It Worth It?

Here's how I'd frame the decision after walking hundreds of families through it. The move tends to pay off when your reasons line up with what DFW genuinely delivers — and it tends to disappoint when you're chasing something Texas was never going to give you.

Moving from California to Dallas makes sense if…
  • You want top-rated schools and more house for your money
  • The no-income-tax math meaningfully changes your family's budget
  • You're targeting a family suburb, not downtown Dallas
  • You can make peace with hot summers and more driving
  • You'd rather own a bigger home than rent a smaller one near the coast

If three or more of those are a clear yes, you're exactly the family DFW tends to work out beautifully for. If walkability and mild weather sit at the very top of your list, be honest with yourself about that before you commit — it's a real factor, and I'd rather you weigh it now.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is moving from California to Dallas worth it?

For many families, yes — especially when the move points you toward the right suburb rather than assuming Dallas proper is the default fit. The math favors Texas: no state income tax, a roughly $7,100 bigger take-home on a $100K salary, and far more house per dollar. You trade mild weather, the ocean, and walkability for that.

How much money do you save moving from California to Texas?

On a $100,000 salary you keep about $7,100 more per year in Texas, since there is no state income tax (CNBC, 2025). The bigger savings is housing — a DFW suburb home often costs less than half of a comparable California metro home, which changes your whole monthly budget.

Do Californians regret moving to Texas?

Some do — but it's usually an expectations mismatch, not a Texas problem. The people who regret it often expected coastal weather and a walkable city, then landed somewhere that didn't fit their daily life. Families who research schools, commute, and suburb fit first rarely regret it.

What are the best Dallas suburbs for families moving from California?

Frisco, Flower Mound, Allen, and McKinney are the four that come up most with California families. All four pair A or A+ school districts with strong family scores. The right one depends on your budget, your commute, and whether you want an established town or new construction.

Are property taxes higher in Texas than California?

Yes — effective property tax rates in many DFW areas (roughly 1.8%–2.2%) run higher than in California, where the base rate is generally 1% plus voter-approved local debt. But Texas has no state income tax, and the homestead exemption removes $140,000 from your home's school-taxable value, which offsets a meaningful share of the difference.

At the end of the day, "worth it" isn't a number — it's whether the trade fits your family. If the schools, the space, and the lower cost of living matter more than the coast and the cooler summers, the Dallas suburbs are one of the best moves a family can make.
