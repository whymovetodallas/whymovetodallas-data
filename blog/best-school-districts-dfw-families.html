Best School Districts in DFW for Families (2026): Frisco, Plano, Carroll/Southlake, Prosper, Highland Park & More

Kristen Carpentier Licensed TX Realtor®  ·  June 2026  ·  15 min read

Last updated June 2026. This guide is reviewed and updated annually as new TEA accountability ratings, Niche rankings, enrollment data, and DFW housing-market conditions are released.

If you are moving to Dallas-Fort Worth with kids, the school district question usually arrives before the house question — and for good reason. In Texas your home address decides your child's public school, so the district you choose quietly shapes the next decade of your family's life. I talk with relocating parents every week who have a job offer in hand and a moving date on the calendar, but no idea whether Frisco, Plano, Prosper, Southlake, or one of the strong North Texas districts further out is the right fit for the children they are actually raising.

This guide covers the districts families ask me about most — and a wider look at the full DFW field — what the 2026 ratings really say, and how to match a district to your child instead of just chasing a ranking.

Quick answer: Carroll ISD is the highest-ranked Dallas-Fort Worth district on Niche's 2026 list, but the best school district for a relocating family depends on budget, commute, home style, and the specific campus zone. Frisco, Prosper, Plano, Highland Park, Coppell, Grapevine-Colleyville, Allen, Lovejoy, and Argyle are all strong districts families commonly compare when moving to the DFW area with kids.

How Texas school ratings actually work

Two scorecards matter when families research public schools in North Texas. The first is the Texas Education Agency (TEA) accountability rating, an A through F grade (scored 0–100) based on student achievement, school progress, and how well a district closes gaps between student groups. The second is Niche, which blends state test data with academics, teacher quality, and tens of thousands of parent and student reviews into a letter grade and a national rank.

TEA and Niche measure different things, and it is worth understanding the distinction. TEA is standardized and accountability-based — the same state metrics apply to every Texas district, and it is the official government rating. Niche is broader and more consumer-facing; it incorporates subjective reviews and can shift year to year when methodology changes. Both are useful, but neither tells the full story on its own. I walk families through both because a district might hold a strong Niche grade based on community reputation while a newer district earns higher TEA scores on raw academic output — and those two things can point to different homes.

One thing I always tell parents: a district-wide A is reassuring, but your child attends one campus, not the whole district. A large A-rated district can still contain a range of campuses, and a smaller district can be remarkably consistent across all schools. Use the district grade to build your short list, then look hard at the specific elementary, middle, and high school that serves the neighborhood you are considering. Feeder patterns matter too, especially in large districts like Frisco, Plano, Lewisville, and McKinney, where one neighborhood feeds a highly sought-after elementary while a nearby neighborhood in the same district feeds a different campus entirely.

Carroll ISD (Southlake): the small district that ranks first

Carroll ISD serves a little over 8,000 students across 11 schools and holds the top spot on Niche's 2026 DFW list. The district earned a 95 out of 100 (A) in the 2025 TEA accountability ratings, and Carroll Senior High is commonly reported with a graduation rate at or above 99 percent at the campus level. What stands out to families is the combination: rigorous academics, nationally competitive athletics, and award-winning fine arts, all inside a district small enough that families feel known from elementary through graduation.

The trade-off is cost. Southlake homes commonly start near $900,000 and climb past $2 million, so Carroll tends to suit families whose budget fits that range and who want everything concentrated in one small district. Highland Park ISD: prestige close to the city

Highland Park ISD enrolls roughly 6,300 students and is one of the smallest, most consistently excellent districts in the metro, earning an A+ from Niche and ranking ninth in DFW on the 2026 Niche list. It is the choice for buyers who want a top-rated district without leaving central Dallas. Entry prices are among the highest in the metro — often $1.2 million and well beyond — so it tends to suit families prioritizing proximity to downtown, Uptown, or Park Cities employment over square footage and yard space.

For families chasing newer construction or more land, the math usually points north toward Frisco, Prosper, or another North Texas suburb with strong schools at a lower price point. Explore our Highland Park neighborhood guide for the community and housing overview.

Frisco ISD: the all-rounder most families land on

Frisco ISD is the district I recommend most often to relocating families, and the numbers explain why. It serves roughly 65,000 students across 75-plus campuses, making it the largest A-rated district in Texas — a distinction the district earned under the 2024–25 TEA accountability ratings, with a four-year graduation rate around 95 percent. The district built schools quickly during Frisco's growth years, so buildings and facilities are newer than in older suburbs. Homes generally run from the mid $400,000s to around $900,000, giving families real choices across price points and neighborhoods.

One thing that sets Frisco ISD apart: its smaller high school model. Rather than consolidating into a handful of enormous campuses, Frisco has built more high schools of moderate size, which tends to give students more opportunities in athletics, fine arts, and student leadership than they might get at a 5,000-student mega-campus. If you want strong academics, modern facilities, and a range of price points, Frisco belongs near the top of your list.

Frisco ISD is the district I recommend most often to families relocating from out of state. The combination of A-rated academics, modern campuses, and a wide range of home prices is hard to beat anywhere in the metro.

Prosper ISD: space, growth, and high-performing schools

Prosper ISD now serves more than 34,000 students — making it one of the fastest-growing districts in North Texas in absolute terms. The district earned an A from TEA with a score of 91 out of 100 in the latest accountability ratings, and it ranks tenth in DFW on Niche's 2026 list. Because Prosper is still building out, families find newer homes from roughly $500,000 to $1 million or more on larger lots than they would get further south. For a growing family that wants newer construction, room to breathe, and excellent schools without Southlake pricing, Prosper is often the sweet spot.

Go in with realistic expectations: rapid enrollment growth means ongoing construction, rezoning and changing feeder patterns as new campuses open, and longer drives to established retail or Dallas employment centers. If your kids are in elementary school, the growth can be manageable. If you have a high-schooler, check the current feeder pattern carefully — boundaries have shifted in recent years and will shift again. Read our full guide to living in Prosper.

Plano ISD: the established option

Plano ISD serves about 46,000 students, holds an A-range Niche grade, and ranks eleventh in DFW on Niche's 2026 list. Its most recent TEA accountability rating was a B at 82 out of 100. Plano is more established than Frisco or Prosper, which shows up as mature neighborhoods, generous tree canopies, deep course catalogs — including robust AP, dual-credit, and career-track programs — and a wider price range that includes more homes under $500,000. Families who value a settled community and a proven track record often prefer it over a newer, faster-growing suburb.

Other DFW school districts worth considering

The best Dallas-Fort Worth school districts extend well beyond the top five. Families willing to look at the wider DFW school district map often find excellent options with lower home prices, shorter commutes, or a better lifestyle match.

Grapevine-Colleyville ISD — Ranked #2 in DFW on Niche's 2026 list. Consistently A-rated and often called one of the most underrated districts in the metro. Serves Grapevine and Colleyville, with strong academics and proximity to DFW airport — an especially practical choice for families with frequent-travel jobs or corporate transferees based at regional headquarters near the airport corridor.

Coppell ISD — Ranked #3 in DFW on Niche 2026. A smaller A-rated district with tight-knit campuses and strong academic consistency. Homes tend to run $600,000 to $1 million-plus, and Coppell's location near LBJ Freeway, the Tollway, and DFW airport makes it practical for families commuting to Irving, Las Colinas, or corporate campuses along that corridor.

Allen ISD — Ranked #4 in DFW on Niche 2026, A-rated by TEA. Strong academics, well-regarded athletics, and homes generally in the $450,000 to $800,000 range. Families on the edge of Frisco's price ceiling often land here — and most are satisfied with the trade.

Lovejoy ISD (Lucas/Fairview) — Ranked #5 in DFW on Niche 2026. A small, A-rated district in the Lucas and Fairview area with a tight community feel and strong academic outcomes. Home prices tend to run $700,000 to $1.5 million-plus. See our Lucas guide and Fairview guide for more.

Argyle ISD — Ranked #6 in DFW on Niche 2026. A small, fast-growing A-rated district northwest of the metro in Denton County. Appeals to families who want a semi-rural feel, newer construction, and a district where everyone still knows each other by name. Home prices are generally more accessible than Southlake or Lovejoy.

Wylie ISD — Ranked #7 in DFW on Niche 2026. A-rated, east of the metro, and an undervalued option for families priced out of Allen or McKinney. Strong academics at a noticeably lower price point.

Keller ISD — A large, A-rated district in north Tarrant County. Solid academics, strong athletics programs, and home prices generally more accessible than most Collin County counterparts. Practical for families whose job centers are in Fort Worth, Alliance, or the mid-cities corridor.

Lewisville ISD — A large Denton County district serving Flower Mound, Highland Village, The Colony, and Lewisville itself. A-range academics and broader price access than most Collin County options, with many homes available under $600,000. Our Flower Mound guide covers one of the strongest communities within LISD.

McKinney ISD — A large, A-rated district serving one of the fastest-growing cities in Texas. Newer campuses, solid academics, and home prices that still offer meaningful value compared to Frisco or Prosper — many quality homes in the $450,000 to $750,000 range.

Hurst-Euless-Bedford ISD (HEB ISD) — Consistently A- or B-rated, serving the mid-cities corridor between Dallas and Fort Worth. Home prices in Euless, Hurst, and Bedford are among the most accessible in the metro — often under $450,000. A strong value option for families with jobs near DFW airport or corporate campuses in the mid-cities.

Rockwall ISD — An A-rated district east of Dallas serving one of the metro's most scenic communities on Lake Ray Hubbard. Home prices are generally lower than Collin County options. The trade-off is the commute into Dallas or the northern suburbs.

What school rankings do not tell you

Niche and TEA ratings are genuinely useful, but there is a meaningful gap between a district grade and the daily reality your child will experience. Here is what rankings leave out — and what I walk families through before they get serious about a neighborhood.

Whether your specific address feeds to the campus you expect. A district grade is an average across all campuses. Two homes on the same street can feed different elementary schools with different performance profiles. Always verify the assigned campus for any address you are considering, not just the district.

Whether boundaries are about to change. In fast-growing districts like Frisco and Prosper, new campuses open every few years and boundaries are redrawn to balance enrollment. The feeder pattern for a home you buy today may look different in three to five years. If you have a child in middle school or high school, this matters more than it does for a kindergartner.

Whether your child's specific program is available at that campus. Gifted and talented programs, dual-language tracks, STEM academies, IB programs, and magnet options are not uniformly distributed across every campus in a district. A highly rated district may not offer your child's specific program at the campus serving your neighborhood. This requires a direct call to the district.

Special education and support services. Families with children who receive IEP services, 504 accommodations, or specialized support need to go beyond district grades entirely. Campus-level resources, caseload sizes, and program quality vary significantly and are not reflected in any published ranking.

Class sizes and school culture. A district's aggregate score says nothing about whether a specific campus is overcrowded, whether the principal is strong, or whether the school culture matches how your child learns and socializes.

Commute reality. A 25-minute difference in the school run each way changes daily family life more than most parents anticipate when searching from out of state. Run the actual commute from both the school and your prospective workplace before falling in love with a neighborhood.

College and career readiness depth. AP, dual-credit, concurrent enrollment, and career and technical education (CTE) programs vary by campus even within the same A-rated district. If your child is in high school or close to it, ask specifically about what is available at their assigned high school.

Best DFW school districts by family situation

Quick answer: There is no single best DFW school district for all relocating families. Carroll ISD in Southlake tops the rankings, but Frisco ISD serves more families well because of its scale, price range, and modern campuses. Prosper ISD is the fastest-growing district and suits families who want newer construction. Plano ISD suits families who want an established community at a lower price point. The right choice depends on your budget, commute, what your child needs, and which specific campus zone the home you can afford falls into.

  • Best overall academic reputation: Carroll ISD (Southlake) — the benchmark for families who want it all in one place
  • Best central Dallas option: Highland Park ISD — top-tier district, 10–15 minutes from Uptown and downtown
  • Best large district for relocating families: Frisco ISD — modern campuses, wide range of home prices, A-rated at scale
  • Best for newer homes and growth areas: Prosper ISD or Argyle ISD — newer communities, master-planned neighborhoods, high-performing schools
  • Best established suburban option: Plano ISD — proven track record, mature neighborhoods, more homes under $500,000 than most A-rated Collin County districts
  • Best value alternatives for strong academics: Allen ISD, Wylie ISD, Keller ISD, McKinney ISD — each A-rated, each with more accessible home prices
  • Best for affordability plus solid schools: HEB ISD, Lewisville ISD, Rockwall ISD — B+ to A range ratings with entry prices below $450,000 in many neighborhoods
  • Best small-district feel: Carroll ISD, Lovejoy ISD, Argyle ISD, Coppell ISD — tight-knit communities where students are not just a number

School district boundaries do not follow city lines

This is one of the most important things I tell every relocating family, and it catches people off guard every time. In Dallas-Fort Worth, school district boundaries frequently do not match the city you think you are buying in.

A home with a Frisco mailing address may not be zoned to Frisco ISD — parts of the city of Frisco actually feed into Prosper ISD or Little Elm ISD depending on the specific street. A home in McKinney may fall into Allen ISD. Parts of what people think of as "Plano" feed into Frisco ISD. And in large, fast-growing districts like Prosper and Frisco, boundary rezoning happens frequently as new campuses come online, which means the feeder pattern for a home you buy today may change in two or three years.

The only way to know for certain is to look up the exact property address in the district's boundary tool or call the district directly. I do this for every client as part of the home search process. Never assume a school district based on city name, zip code, or neighborhood marketing materials — always verify by address before you make an offer.

Common school-search mistakes families make when moving to DFW

After helping hundreds of families navigate this process, these are the errors I see most often — and they are easy to avoid once you know to look for them.

Mistakes to avoid
  • Assuming city name equals school district. This is the most dangerous mistake in DFW. Zip codes and city names are not reliable indicators of the school district. Verify by address.
  • Choosing only by district rank. The #1 ranked district may not offer your child's specific program, may not be near homes in your budget, or may not have the culture that fits your family. Rankings are a starting point, not a conclusion.
  • Forgetting to check the assigned high school. Elementary is often consistent across a district. High schools vary more in course offerings, culture, athletics, and feeder-to-college outcomes. Look closely at the specific campus, not just the district grade.
  • Ignoring commute. Moving to DFW from out of state often involves a mental reset on driving distances. Commute patterns in DFW can add 45–60 minutes to your day compared to what you are used to, especially during peak hours.
  • Ignoring rezoning risk in fast-growth districts. Prosper ISD, Frisco ISD, and McKinney ISD have all redrawn boundaries in recent years and will do so again. If your child is in high school or close to it, confirm the current feeder pattern and ask whether any boundary changes are expected.
  • Assuming a transfer will be approved. Inter-district transfers in Texas are approved at the district's discretion and are often limited or unavailable. Never buy a home counting on a transfer being granted.
  • Comparing homes without looking at total monthly cost. Property tax rates, HOA dues, MUD/PID assessments, and commute costs can change the real monthly payment significantly. I walk clients through the full cost picture as part of every relocation conversation.

Quick school terms to know before moving to Texas

For families relocating from other states, some of the language around Texas public schools is unfamiliar. Here are the basics:

  • ISD — Independent School District. Every public school system in Texas is an ISD. Frisco ISD, Plano ISD, Carroll ISD, etc.
  • Attendance zone — The specific geographic area assigned to a particular school. Your home address determines your attendance zone, and therefore your assigned campus.
  • Feeder pattern — The path a student follows from elementary to middle school to high school. A home in elementary attendance zone A may feed to middle school B and high school C. Always trace the full pattern.
  • TEA rating — The Texas Education Agency accountability grade for a district or campus, issued annually. Grades run A (90–100) to F (below 70), based on student achievement, school progress, and closing the gaps between student groups.
  • MUD/PID — Municipal Utility District or Public Improvement District. A common assessment in newer master-planned communities that funds infrastructure, often adding $200–$400 per month to the true cost of ownership on top of the property tax rate.
  • Transfer — A request to attend a school outside your assigned attendance zone. Approval is at the district's discretion, is often limited, and is never guaranteed. Do not make a home purchase decision contingent on a transfer being approved.

How to choose the right Dallas-Fort Worth school district for your child

Here is the order I walk families through. First, set your honest housing budget — that alone narrows the field significantly, and that is not a problem. Second, name what your child actually needs: a competitive student-athlete, a kid who needs robust special education services, a future engineer who wants STEM and dual-credit options, or a creative who needs serious fine arts and theater. Third, look at the specific campuses serving the homes in your price range, not just the district letter grade. Fourth, factor commute — a 25-minute difference each way changes daily family life more than most parents expect when they are searching from out of state. Fifth, look at total monthly cost, not just purchase price, especially in newer communities with MUD or PID assessments.

Match those five factors and the right district usually becomes clear. The highest-ranked district on any list is not automatically the right one for your family. The right one is the district where your child will actually thrive — and where the daily reality of getting there, affording the home, and building a life makes sense for your whole household.

Frequently asked questions about DFW school districts

What is the best school district in DFW for 2026?

By Niche's 2026 ranking, Carroll ISD in Southlake is the top-rated district in Dallas-Fort Worth. Other high-performing districts relocating families often compare include Grapevine-Colleyville ISD (#2), Coppell ISD (#3), Allen ISD (#4), Lovejoy ISD (#5), Argyle ISD (#6), Wylie ISD (#7), Frisco ISD (#8), Highland Park ISD (#9), Prosper ISD (#10), and Plano ISD (#11). The best district for your family depends on budget, your child's needs, commute, and the specific campus zone — the highest-ranked district is not automatically the right one for you.

Is Frisco ISD or Plano ISD better for families relocating to Texas?

Both are excellent. Frisco ISD is the largest A-rated district in Texas with newer schools and a wide range of home prices, which appeals to families wanting modern campuses and real choices. Plano ISD is more established, with mature neighborhoods, deep course offerings, and more homes under $500,000. Newer and growing points to Frisco; settled and proven points to Plano.

Which DFW district gives the most value for the money?

For families balancing strong schools against price, Frisco ISD and Prosper ISD are often strong value options with homes from the $450,000s to around $1 million and A-rated schools. Districts like Allen ISD, Wylie ISD, Keller ISD, McKinney ISD, Lewisville ISD, HEB ISD, and Rockwall ISD may also be worth comparing depending on commute, budget, and campus zone.

Do I have to live inside the district to attend its schools?

In Texas, public school assignment is based on your home address, so you generally need to live within a district's boundaries — and often within a specific campus zone — to attend. Some districts offer limited inter-district transfers, but you should never assume one will be approved. Always confirm the exact district and campus zone for any home before you make an offer.

Are school districts the same as city boundaries in DFW?

No. School district boundaries frequently cross city lines in Dallas-Fort Worth. A home with a Frisco, Plano, McKinney, Little Elm, or Prosper mailing address may not be zoned to the district you assume. Always verify the district and campus assignment by street address — not city name, not zip code, not neighborhood marketing materials.

What is the best DFW school district for new construction?

Prosper ISD, Frisco ISD, Argyle ISD, and parts of McKinney ISD are popular with buyers wanting newer homes and strong schools. The trade-off is that fast-growing districts may have more rezoning, new campus construction, and changing feeder patterns than older, built-out districts. If your child is in middle or high school, feeder stability matters more than it does for a kindergartner.

Which DFW school districts are best if I want more affordable homes?

Families who want strong schools at lower entry prices than Southlake or Highland Park most often compare Frisco ISD, Plano ISD, Allen ISD, Wylie ISD, Keller ISD, McKinney ISD, Lewisville ISD, Rockwall ISD, and Hurst-Euless-Bedford ISD. The right fit depends on commute, budget, and the specific campus zone at any given property.

Should I choose a district based only on rankings?

No. Rankings are useful for building a short list, but the right district depends on your child's specific needs, the campus zone for homes in your budget, your commute, and your overall lifestyle fit. A lower-ranked district may be the better daily-life match. Use rankings to eliminate clear mismatches, then look hard at the specifics — campus ratings, feeder patterns, programs available, and the actual homes in your price range inside that zone.
