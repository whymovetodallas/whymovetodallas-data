Buying a House in Dallas From Out of State? Check These 5 Records First

Listing photos show you the house. They can't show you the tax bill, the HOA, the flood map, or the school the address is actually zoned to. Here is the homework I do on a DFW house before my clients write an offer.

Kristen CarpentierLicensed TX Realtor®  ·  November 6, 2026  ·  19 min read

If you're buying a house in Dallas from out of state, you're probably doing most of your house hunting from listing photos, late at night, a few states away. My husband and I made this same move from Arizona, so I know exactly what that feels like. The photos are how you find a house you love. They just can't tell you what you're actually buying.

So I'm going to walk you through it the way I do it for my clients, using a real house I helped a family buy in Colleyville. When I pulled the appraisal district record, the property taxes came from five different taxing units, and the tax figure on the listing was from an older year. The public listing mentioned open enrollment to Grapevine-Colleyville ISD, while the school fields on that same listing were all Keller ISD schools. None of that means anyone did anything wrong. Records don't always agree with each other, and that's exactly why I go through every one of them.

Quick answer: Before you make an offer on a Dallas Fort Worth house from out of state, check five records for that exact address: the seller's disclosure and FEMA flood map, the full tax bill on the appraisal district site, the HOA's resale certificate, the school assignment confirmed with the district, and the drive at the times you'd really be driving it.

How a Listing Is Built (and What It Leaves Out)

A listing is written to sell the house, and that's normal. That's its job. The photos, the description, and the words the agent picks are all there to get you excited enough to come see it. I want my buyers excited too. I just want them excited about a house that also checks out when we look at the paperwork.

In our local MLS, the system agents use to list homes here, every listing has two sides. There's the public side you see on sites like Realtor.com, and there's an agent side with notes only agents can read, called the private remarks. On this Colleyville house, the two sides said different things about schools.

Public side (what you see)"Top rated schools" and "Open enrollment to GCISD"Quoted from the public listing remarks. The school fields on the same listing named Keller ISD campuses.
Agent side (private remarks)Buyer told to verify the information, including schoolsParaphrased, never quoted. A careful, common note, and it pointed straight at the thing worth checking.

So the heads up was right there in the listing. It was just on the side the buyer never gets to see. That's a big reason I read both sides of every listing for my clients, especially when they're shopping from out of state and can't drive by and ask the neighbors. The good news is that most of what you need to check sits in public records or in documents you have a right to ask for.

Record 1: The Seller's Disclosure and the Flood Map

In Texas, the seller of a house like this has to give the buyer a written notice about the property's condition, under Texas Property Code Section 5.008. It asks about past flooding, drainage, foundation repairs, and the age and type of the roof. The form itself says it is a disclosure of the seller's knowledge as of the date the seller signed it, and that it is not a substitute for any inspections or warranties. That matters, because a seller can honestly check "no" on something they never knew about.

If you're looking at brand new construction, a new home that has never been lived in isn't required to come with this disclosure (the statute exempts it). On a new build, your own third-party inspection carries even more weight. I'm not sure I've had a single new construction client who wasn't grateful they did a separate inspection.

A disclosure is a snapshot

On our Colleyville house, the seller signed the disclosure about 13 months before our buyers received it, because the house had been on the market for a long stretch. Anything that happened after the signing date might not be on it.

The flood map check passed

The disclosure has a whole section on flooding, so the next thing I do is look the address up on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center, which you can do yourself, from anywhere, for free. This house came back in Zone X, which FEMA describes as an area of minimal flood hazard, and that matched what the seller marked. Zone X doesn't promise a yard will never hold water after a big storm, so drainage still belongs on your inspector's list. I like showing the checks that pass, too. Most records come back fine. What you want to know is which ones don't.

The insurance answers didn't line up

The seller checked yes on the question about filing a flood damage claim, then wrote in an explanation describing water damage from a plumbing leak inside the house. But the question about water damage not caused by a flood, and the one about other insurance claims, were both checked no.

That happens more often than you'd think, because these forms usually get filled out at a kitchen table. It doesn't mean anyone was hiding anything. It tells you what to ask about. On a house like this, I'd ask for the repair invoices and the prior inspection report the seller listed, and for the home's insurance claims history, called a CLUE report. The Texas Department of Insurance says that if you're buying a house, you can ask the owner to show you one. It lists claims filed on the property for the past seven years, even claims from before the current owner.

The roof age was "unknown"

This house is a little over 10 years old, and the disclosure marked the roof age as unknown. The seller had owned it for a little over a year when they filled out the form, so that makes sense. It's still worth finding out, because hail is a real part of life in North Texas. A May 2026 KVUE report republished by the National Insurance Crime Bureau called Texas the hail damage capital of the country for the 11th straight year.

With some homeowners policies, how much you get paid for a damaged roof depends on how old it is. The Texas Department of Insurance explains that an actual cash value policy pays less as the roof gets older, while a replacement cost policy pays the same no matter its age. Before you budget for insurance, find out how old the roof is and which kind of policy you're being quoted.

Record 2: The Full Tax Bill

This is where out-of-state buyers often get the biggest surprise. Texas has no state property tax. Your bill comes from the local taxing units where the house sits, like the county, the city, and the school district, and each one sets its own rate (the Texas Comptroller's property tax basics walk through how that works). For our Colleyville house, the Tarrant Appraisal District property search listed five of them. You can look up any address there yourself before you ever write an offer.

Keller ISD was the biggest piece, at a little over 1% on its own. Now remember the tax figure on the listing? It matched an older year's bill, and the most recent bill in the records was about 17% higher. Those past bills also reflect the seller's exemptions, not yours. Your own bill will depend on your own homestead filing after you move in.

Why does that matter? Your property taxes usually get folded into your monthly payment through escrow. On this house, using an older tax figure would have understated the payment your lender quoted by well over $100 a month.

MUDs and PIDs

You'll also hear about a MUD (municipal utility district) or a PID (public improvement district), which can add their own tax or assessment on top of the regular bill. On this house, the appraisal record didn't show a special district among the taxing units, but a PID assessment isn't always on that list. In Texas, the seller owes you a notice if the house is in one, so ask for it and put it in your budget if it applies.

I wrote a step-by-step walkthrough of checking an address this way in Texas property tax for out-of-state buyers, including how the homestead exemption changes your number.

Record 3: The HOA

Photos will never show you what the HOA actually costs or what it's dealing with. On this house, I had two different dues numbers from two different documents, plus a fee that only showed up at closing.

The listingabout $800every six months
Seller's disclosureabout $863per six-month period, same house
At closing$200transfer fee to the HOA's management company

A transfer fee is pretty common here, but you'd never see it in a photo. The document that reconciles the current HOA obligations is the resale certificate. Under Texas Property Code Section 207.003, the association generally must deliver it no later than the 10th business day after it receives the written request and verifies the requester's authority. It may require proof of the buyer's right to acquire the property and payment before it starts, and the written request has to say where and to whom the certificate should be sent. The certificate has to show things like the regular assessment, anything owed and unpaid, and any lawsuit the association is part of. The HOA addendum to your contract spells out who orders it and how many days they have, so I always make sure we know who's getting it and when.

Record 4: The School the Address Is Zoned To

This was the biggest surprise on this house, because the real question is which school your kids will attend from this particular address. Most of Colleyville is in Grapevine-Colleyville ISD, but not all of it. The city lines and the school district lines don't match. This house sits in one of the parts of Colleyville served by Keller ISD.

I covered this split in detail in my video Moving to Colleyville TX: Same Street, Different Schools, and in my guide to moving to Colleyville, Texas.

What "open enrollment" means here

So what does "open enrollment to GCISD" mean for a house zoned to Keller ISD? The district's own non-resident student transfer policy says Grapevine-Colleyville ISD is a limited open-enrollment district, and a student who lives in Grapevine or Colleyville but outside the district may apply to attend designated schools.

What GCISD considers for each transfer applicationSummarized from the district's posted policy
  1. Available capacity at the requested school
  2. Availability of instructional staff
  3. The student's attendance, behavior, and grades are in good standing
  4. The student has passed all state-mandated assessments
Applications are considered by student, not by family. A student applies again at each transition year. An approved transfer for one child does not guarantee space for current or future siblings.

The district has changed how this works before. Community Impact covered GCISD's transfer policy changes, and the district's transfer information page describes designated pathways determined by staffing and capacity. For a family with two or three kids, "open enrollment" on a listing really means a set of applications, one for each child, to district-designated pathways. That is a very different thing from buying a house zoned to the school you were hoping for.

Confirm the zoned campus with the district

The Texas Education Agency says it does not collect school attendance zone information and tells families to contact the district. So the district is where the answer comes from. Keller ISD's school locator returned an elementary, an intermediate, a middle, and a high school for this address, but the map behind it was still labeled for the previous school year when I checked. That's why I tell clients to call the district and confirm the assignment for the year their kids will actually start.

Then I check the district's news for anything already decided. Keller ISD's board voted to consolidate schools. Beginning in the 2027 to 2028 school year, elementary schools will serve pre-K through fifth grade, middle schools sixth through eighth, and high schools ninth through twelfth. The intermediate school on this address's locator result is one of three intermediate schools closing after the 2026 to 2027 school year. The district said boundary changes were still to be finalized, and Community Impact reported the closures came down to declining enrollment.

Ask these two questions in writing before the offer

1. Is each child eligible to apply now, which district-designated pathway applies, and when would the district decide the application? Treat an application as pending unless the district has actually approved it.

2. Which Keller ISD campuses are currently assigned, and when will the post-consolidation boundaries be final?

None of that makes it the wrong house. It just means that if schools are a big reason you're choosing a house, the answer has to come from the district before you're committed. If you're still comparing districts, start with my overview of DFW school districts.

What to Check Before the Offer, and During the Option Period

The standard Texas resale contract, TREC's One to Four Family Residential Contract, includes an option period: for an option fee, you get the unrestricted right to terminate the contract within the number of days you negotiate. It's not a set number of days in Texas like it can be in other states. That gives you a natural order for this homework.

Questions Out-of-State Buyers Ask

Can I buy a house in Dallas without seeing it in person?

Many buyers narrow their search remotely. You can check the flood map, full tax bill, school assignment, and drive times from anywhere before an offer. Your inspection, claims history, and HOA resale certificate come during the option period. A local agent can view the house and read the agent-only remarks for you.

How do I find the real property tax bill on a Texas house?

Look up the address on the county appraisal district website and list every taxing unit on the record, then add their published rates. The tax figure on a listing may be from an older year and reflects the seller's exemptions. Your bill depends on your own homestead filing after you move in.

Does open enrollment on a listing guarantee a school?

No. In Grapevine-Colleyville ISD, transfers are applications reviewed for each student based on capacity, staffing, attendance, behavior, grades, and state test results. A student reapplies at each transition year, and one child's approval does not guarantee space for siblings. Confirm the zoned campus with the district.

Does a Texas seller have to disclose past insurance claims?

The Texas seller's disclosure asks about insurance claims, but it reflects only the seller's knowledge as of the date signed. Ask the owner for a CLUE report, which the Texas Department of Insurance says shows claims filed on the property for the past seven years, including claims from before the current owner.

How long does an HOA have to provide a resale certificate in Texas?

Under Texas Property Code Section 207.003, the association generally must deliver it no later than the 10th business day after it receives the written request and verifies the requester's authority. It may require proof of your right to buy and payment first. Your contract's HOA addendum says who orders it and when.
