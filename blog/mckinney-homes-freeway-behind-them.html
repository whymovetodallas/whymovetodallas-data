Buying a Home in McKinney? Check the Road Maps First

A quiet field or tree line can look permanent on a showing day. Before you choose a lot, compare the address with the official transportation plans that could change what is behind it.

Kristen CarpentierLicensed TX Realtor® · October 31, 2026 · 11 min read

A McKinney home can look settled while the land behind it is still part of a changing transportation plan. That does not automatically make the home a bad choice. A future road may improve access for one household and create a construction, noise, or view concern for another. The mistake is deciding from the fence line alone.

Quick answer: Before buying a home in McKinney, check the exact address against TxDOT, NTTA, Collin County, and City of McKinney planning maps. Separate a generalized planning line from a selected route and a funded construction project. Then verify the parcel through title, survey, contract documents, agency records, and a real visit before the deadlines in your agreement.

What the selected US 380 route means

TxDOT selected the Blue Alternative for the US 380 project across north McKinney after its environmental review. The official project description generally connects existing US 380 near Coit Road on the west with existing US 380 near FM 1827 and New Hope Road on the east. A selected alternative is important evidence because it is more specific than an early concept. It still is not a parcel survey, a promise that every segment will be built at once, or a date certain for construction beside a particular home.

The useful buyer question is not simply, “Is there a bypass?” It is, “What does the current official route show near this address, and what stage has that portion reached?” A route can matter before construction begins, but its practical effect depends on distance, grade, barriers, access points, design changes, project delivery, and what your family values.

A line on a map is not a construction schedule

Transportation projects move through multiple decisions. Those decisions can overlap, change, or apply differently to separate segments. Treating every line as a bulldozer-ready project exaggerates the evidence. Treating every unbuilt line as irrelevant ignores the reason the map exists.

1
Planning conceptA city or regional map may reserve or study a general corridor. The alignment can be broad and can move.
2
Selected route or environmental decisionAn agency has chosen an alternative and completed a formal milestone. This narrows the question but does not make every segment construction-ready.
3
Design, right of way, utilities, and fundingThese workstreams can proceed on different timelines. Ask which segment and which phase the source describes.
4
Letting and constructionA funded contract and active construction are different from a future-plan line. Confirm current dates directly with the responsible agency.
Decision rule: record the map date, agency, project name, segment, and stage. If the source does not state a construction date, do not invent one.

The City of McKinney makes the same limitation clear in its thoroughfare planning materials: mapped alignments are generalized, may shift, and are not a site-specific determination. That makes the city map a starting point for a property question, not the final answer.

Source: City of McKinney, 2023 Master Thoroughfare Plan, dated December 19, 2023. The city says future alignments are generalized, may shift during engineering, and are not precise site-specific locations. Checked October 11, 2026.

Seller disclosure is not a future-road search

The standard Texas Real Estate Commission Seller's Disclosure Notice does not contain a question that specifically asks whether a planned highway is near the property. That narrow fact does not decide every seller's legal duties and does not replace your own research. Review the current form and your transaction documents, ask direct questions, and consult the appropriate professional if you need legal advice.

TREC Seller's Disclosure Notice, form 55-1, effective May 28, 2026.

Screen the lot, not just the neighborhood

A neighborhood name can span very different edges, streets, and future conditions. One home may back to an internal greenbelt. Another may sit across open land that appears on a future thoroughfare map. Another may be near a corridor but separated by grade, distance, or existing development. A neighborhood-level answer cannot settle a lot-level decision.

Start with the official Collin Central Appraisal District property search or interactive map to identify the parcel and legal description, then compare that record with the survey and title documents in your transaction. An appraisal map is a research aid, not a boundary survey.

For open land, ask what the source actually establishes. A city plan may show a generalized future road. A zoning case may show a development proposal. A recorded plat or easement may answer a parcel question. These are different documents. Save them separately and write down what each one does not answer.

Then connect the road question to your routine. A corridor that concerns a buyer focused on backyard quiet may appeal to someone who values quicker regional access. Test school, work, airport, medical, and activity routes at the times you expect to use them. Use your contract window deliberately

Do as much planning research as possible before an offer. If your contract includes an option or other due-diligence period, use the time and rights your agreement actually provides. The deadlines and remedies come from the contract, not from a blog checklist, so confirm them with the appropriate professionals for your transaction.

Build one evidence folder for the address

Use current documents. Record the source and date. Keep questions and answers with the property file.

Current agency mapsSave the TxDOT, NTTA, county, and city pages that apply, including legends and project-stage language.
Survey, title, and recorded documentsReview the documents provided in your transaction for easements, reservations, exceptions, and property boundaries. Ask qualified professionals about anything you do not understand.
Direct questionsAsk the seller, listing side, builder, HOA, and responsible agencies specific written questions about the land or route that concerns you.
On-site checksVisit in daylight and after dark, during ordinary traffic, and at the times your household will use nearby roads. Photograph the view and access points for your own comparison.
Your decision ruleWrite down what would make you proceed, renegotiate, seek more advice, or walk away while you still have the rights available under your agreement.

Do not reduce the final choice to distance alone. A line that appears close on a map may have meaningful topography or separation, while a farther interchange may affect daily traffic more. There is no universal “safe distance” that fits every design and household. The goal is not to eliminate every possible future change. It is to replace a surprise with an informed tradeoff.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the US 380 bypass planned in McKinney?

TxDOT's selected Blue Alternative generally runs from existing US 380 near Coit Road across north McKinney to existing US 380 near FM 1827 and New Hope Road. Use TxDOT's current project page and exhibits for the authoritative route, then compare a specific parcel through title, survey, and other applicable records.

Is the US 380 bypass in McKinney under construction?

Do not use one label for the whole corridor. TxDOT completed the environmental decision for the selected alternative, but design, right of way, utilities, funding, letting, and construction can differ by segment and time. Check the current TxDOT project information for the exact segment you are researching.

Will a Texas seller's disclosure tell me about a planned freeway?

The standard TREC Seller's Disclosure Notice does not contain a question specifically asking whether a planned highway is nearby. It is not a substitute for checking official maps, title, survey, recorded documents, and the specific facts of the property. This is general information, not a conclusion about any person's disclosure duties.

Which maps should I check before buying a McKinney home?

Check TxDOT for state highway projects, NTTA for tollway information, Collin County for the Outer Loop, and the City of McKinney for future thoroughfares. Each system answers a different question. Record the agency, map date, project stage, and limits instead of relying on a cropped image from a listing or social post.

Does the McKinney thoroughfare plan show an exact future road location?

Not necessarily. The city describes planned alignments as generalized and subject to change, so the map is a prompt for more research rather than a site-specific boundary determination. Review current city material and parcel-level documents before making a property decision.

Is living near a planned road always a bad idea?

No. A future road can create access benefits and can also create construction, traffic, noise, or view concerns. The balance depends on the final design, project stage, exact parcel, and your household's priorities. Evaluate the tradeoff rather than treating proximity as automatically positive or negative.

McKinney's growth does not make every open field a threat, and a selected corridor does not make every nearby home a mistake. It does mean the view from the back fence is only one piece of evidence. Check the official plans, identify the real project stage, verify the parcel, and make the road question part of your offer decision while you can still act on the answer.
