Moving to DFW With Kids: How to Choose the Right Suburb for Your Family

Kristen Carpentier Licensed TX Realtor®  ·  June 19, 2026  ·  10 min read

If you're moving to DFW with kids, you've probably already opened a map of Dallas–Fort Worth, seen sixty-some suburbs, and felt your stomach drop a little. I've been there. My husband and I moved here from the Phoenix area and raised our four kids in these suburbs, and I've since helped hundreds of families do the same. So let me tell you the thing that makes this whole decision manageable: you're not really choosing a house. You're choosing a daily life: a commute, a school zone, and where your weekends happen. Once you know which area fits your family, the house part gets a lot easier.

Quick answer: To choose the right DFW suburb for your family, rank four levers (schools, commute, budget, and lifestyle) in your own order. Most families nail three of the four; the fourth is the trade-off. Filter the 60+ suburbs against your ranking to land on a shortlist of three, then verify school zoning, test your real commute, and compare total monthly cost before you buy.

It covers the same four-lever method, then goes further than I could in eight minutes: the real budget math, the new-versus-resale call, and the full 30-60-90 relocation timeline I only mention in the video. Start With the Decision, Not the Listings

When you're moving with kids, the stakes of this decision aren't really about square footage. They're about your children's daily life: the school they'll walk into, the friends they'll make, the field they'll practice on, and how much of you they'll get after the commute. So the most common mistake I see families make is opening a listings app on day one and falling for a beautiful house, before they know whether that house is in a district they'd choose, a commute they could live with, or a neighborhood where their kids would actually thrive. It's backwards. In DFW, the smart order is to decide your area first and your house second.

Here's why that matters more here than in most metros: Dallas–Fort Worth is enormous, and "Dallas" rarely means the city of Dallas for relocating families. It usually means the ring of northern suburbs, places like Frisco, Allen, Plano, McKinney, Flower Mound, and Southlake, where many of the school districts and newer housing options relocating families ask me about are concentrated. Each has its own personality, price point, and school district. The good news is you don't have to evaluate all sixty. You just need a method to filter them.

Moving to DFW With Kids: The Four Levers That Narrow 60+ Suburbs to 3

When you're relocating with children, every decision runs through one question (where will my kids actually thrive?), so I anchor the search on four levers and weigh each one through a parent's eyes: schools, commute, budget, and lifestyle. Here's the honest part: you usually get three of the four pretty easily, and the fourth is the one that costs you. If top-tier schools, a short commute, and a brand-new build are all non-negotiable, then budget is the lever that stretches. That's not bad news. That's just the trade, and knowing your trade up front is what keeps you from spinning in circles for six months while your kids wait to find out where they're starting school.

Lever 1: Schools

For most families, this is lever number one. Three things to know. First, in Texas your address sets your schools. You're zoned to a district, and even nearby homes can land in different attendance zones, so never assume; verify the exact zoning for the exact address using the district's official attendance-zone lookup, not just a listing site. Second, ratings are a starting point, not the final answer, so use sites like GreatSchools and Niche to build a shortlist of districts, then dig deeper. Third, fit is kid-specific: the "best" district on paper isn't always best for your child. Younger kids give you flexibility, but if you have a middle- or high-schooler, research the specific campus and feeder pattern, because a child who needs a strong special-education program, a serious arts department, or a competitive athletics track may be better served by the A district with that program than the A+ district without it. The northern suburbs make this easy because so many A and A+ districts sit within a 40-minute radius, so compare a few on the DFW school districts hub or in my guide to the best DFW school districts for families.

A Compliance Note from Kristen

As a Realtor, my job isn't to steer you. I won't rank suburbs by "safety" or tell you which area is "nicest." Instead I point you to the tools so you can decide for yourself: for schools, GreatSchools and Niche; for safety, NeighborhoodScout, City-Data, and your local police department's crime map. You look at the real data and make the call.

Lever 2: Commute

This is the one that surprises my out-of-state families most: DFW is way bigger than it looks, and it is not "all just Dallas." A drive that's twenty minutes at noon can double at 8 a.m. So if anyone in your house has to be somewhere (an office, a campus, a hospital), start there and build outward, and test your true rush-hour commute, not the easy Sunday version. Common employer anchors families build around include Legacy West and the rest of Plano, Las Colinas, downtown Dallas, the DFW Airport corridor, Richardson, and Fort Worth. Many northern routes use tollways (the Dallas North Tollway, the President George Bush Turnpike, SH 121), so factor those in. And if you're a two-job household, look for the center of gravity between you; there's often a better middle-ground suburb than people expect. With kids, the commute lever is really about time: every extra twenty minutes each way is time you're not at the dinner table, the practice field, or school pickup. Map your drive against your kids' school start times and activity schedules, not just your office hours. A "great" commute that makes you miss every 5 p.m. game isn't great for your family.

Lever 3: Budget

Budget is where families get half the picture right. Yes, Texas has no state income tax, which is real money back every paycheck if you're coming from a high-tax state. But the money tends to move to the property-tax column, and total monthly cost, not list price, is what actually matters. With kids, budget also means room to grow: enough bedrooms as the family expands, a yard for them to actually use, and a cushion for the kid line items (youth sports fees, activities, childcare or after-school care) that never show up on a listing but hit your monthly number all the same. More on the real math in the next section, because it deserves its own breakdown.

Lever 4: Lifestyle

Lifestyle is what makes a family stay. Do you want a walkable town square, like Southlake? Trails and lake access, like Flower Mound next to Lake Grapevine? Big master-planned amenities and deep youth sports, like Frisco? Or established, tree-lined neighborhoods with historic charm, like downtown McKinney? None is better than another; they're just different daily lives. Rank what your family actually wants, because this lever is what your kids will remember.

Write your four-lever ranking down before you tour a single home. The families who do this move with confidence; the ones who don't tour twenty houses across six suburbs and end up more confused than when they started.

Run the Real Numbers: Total Monthly Cost

Here's the money mistake that quietly makes families overpay every month, and it's not the one you'd guess: a cheaper house with a higher tax load and higher insurance can cost more per month than a pricier house down the road. So always compare the total monthly number, not the list price.

Texas has no state income tax. For a household coming from California, New York, or Illinois (states that all tax wage income, with Illinois at a 4.95% flat rate and California and New York on progressive brackets, plus a New York City surcharge where it applies), that's a meaningful, every-paycheck difference. How much depends on your household income, so compare your own numbers. On the home side, the Texas homestead exemption removes $140,000 from your home's school-taxable value (Texas Tax Code §11.13(b)) once you file on your primary residence. The offset is property taxes, which often run higher than out-of-state buyers expect, especially in newer master-planned areas with Municipal Utility District (MUD) or Public Improvement District (PID) assessments layered on top. I never quote a fixed percentage, because it varies by the exact home and neighborhood; the rule is to run the real numbers for the specific address.

And budget for the line items that aren't the mortgage. This is hail country: our homeowner's insurance treats your roof like a piñata, and wind/hail deductibles are often percentage-based (commonly around 1%–2% of the home's value), so get a quote for the exact home. Summer electricity spikes are real too: a 2,500–3,500 sq ft home can run $250–$400 a month to cool from June through September. Add tolls for most northern commutes. From 60+ Suburbs to Your Top 3

Let me make the four-lever method concrete, the way I did in the video. Say one of you has an office in Plano, you've got two school-age kids, your budget is in the $800s, and you really want a newer home. Watch what happens: the commute lever pulls you toward Plano or just north of it; schools narrow your list inside that band; the newer-home lever nudges you toward the growth areas; and your budget tells you which of those is actually realistic. Suddenly you're not staring at forty suburbs; you're looking at three, maybe Plano, Frisco, and Allen. Different family, different ranking, different three. That's the whole point: there's no single "best" suburb, only the best fit for how you ranked the levers. And don't stop at the obvious names; depending on your priorities, suburbs like Coppell, Prosper, Argyle, and Colleyville are worth a look too.

New Construction vs. Resale

So many families relocating with kids are drawn to the shiny master-planned communities up north, and they're wonderful. Just know the trade. New construction comes with a builder-drafted contract, so you want someone on your side reading it before you sign, and incentives should be weighed against the full long-term cost, not just the upfront perk. Those newer growth areas can also carry higher MUD or PID taxes while infrastructure gets paid off. Resale homes in established suburbs often give you more mature neighborhoods, bigger trees, and a different cost structure, but it still comes down to the exact home. Neither is better. It's the four levers again. If new construction is on your radar, my guide to mistakes families make moving to Dallas covers the contract traps to watch for.

Your 30-60-90 Relocation Timeline

This is the part I only mention in the video, because it deserves a real walkthrough. Most family relocations fall into one of three runways: a fast track (corporate transfers often move in 30–60 days), a standard window (three to six months, the most common), and a long runway (six to twelve months, common for remote workers and growing families with time to plan). Whichever you're on, the rhythm below keeps you moving in the right order. Earlier starts simply give you more school and inventory options.

Before you fly · the runway

Rank, budget, shortlist

Rank your four levers as a family and write them down. Set a realistic budget by total monthly cost, not list price. Get pre-approved with a lender who knows Texas property taxes. Then use the Suburb Matcher to turn your priorities into a top three, so when you land, you tour a shortlist, not a metro.

First 30 days

Tour your shortlist in person

Visit your top three suburbs, ideally on a weekday so you can test the true rush-hour commute. Verify the exact school zoning for any address you love; don't assume. Decide whether you'll rent first to learn the area or buy now; both are valid depending on your runway and how confident you are in your shortlist.

Days 30–60

Narrow to one suburb and make an offer

Pick your suburb and two or three target neighborhoods, then make an offer. In Texas you'll typically negotiate a short option period (often around 7–10 days) for inspections, and use it. If you're buying new construction, have your agent review the builder contract and incentives before you sign anything.

Days 60–90

Close, enroll, and settle in

Close on the home, then file your homestead exemption on your primary residence to capture that $140,000 school-value reduction. Set up utilities, and enroll your kids, and bring records, transcripts, and immunizations so registration goes smoothly. Budget the first month for tolls, summer electricity, and hail-ready insurance.

Quick relocation checklist
  • Four levers ranked and written down before touring
  • Budget set by total monthly cost (taxes, tolls, insurance, summer electric included)
  • Pre-approval from a Texas-savvy lender
  • School zoning verified by exact address
  • True rush-hour commute tested in person
  • Homestead exemption filed after closing
  • School records ready for enrollment
Before you make an offer: due diligence
  • Confirm official school zoning for the exact address with the district
  • Pull the county appraisal district (CAD) tax record for the home
  • Check for MUD or PID assessments and any HOA dues
  • Get an insurance quote for the specific home (ask about roof age and the wind/hail deductible)
  • Drive the real commute at your actual departure time
  • Gather enrollment documents (records, transcripts, immunizations)

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I choose the right DFW suburb for my family?

Rank four levers in your family's order: schools, commute, budget, and lifestyle. Most families get three of the four easily, and the fourth is the trade-off. Write your ranking down before touring, then filter the 60+ DFW suburbs against it to land on a shortlist of three.

What is the best DFW suburb for families?

There's no single best suburb, only the best fit for how you rank schools, commute, budget, and lifestyle. Frisco, Allen, Plano, McKinney, Flower Mound, and Southlake all come up often, but the right one depends on your priorities. The Suburb Matcher turns your ranking into a personalized top three.

How long does it take to relocate to DFW with kids?

Corporate transfers often move in 30–60 days, while families with more runway take three to twelve months. A workable rhythm is a pre-move planning phase, then a 30-60-90 day window to tour, make an offer, close, and enroll kids. Starting earlier gives you more school and inventory options.

Should I choose the school district or the house first?

In DFW, the district lines are the map. Pick the two or three districts that fit your family first, then look at homes only inside those boundaries. In Texas your address sets your schools, so verify the exact zoning for the exact home, since nearby houses can fall in different attendance zones.

How much should I budget for a family home in the DFW suburbs?

Medians range widely: roughly $498K in McKinney, $516K in Allen, $526K in Plano, $622K in Flower Mound, and $679K in Frisco. Budget by total monthly cost: taxes, tolls, hail insurance, and summer electricity add up.

Which DFW suburbs should families compare first?

Many relocating families start by comparing Frisco, Allen, Plano, McKinney, Flower Mound, Coppell, and Southlake because they offer different mixes of school districts, commute patterns, price points, and lifestyle options. The right shortlist depends on your exact priorities and address-specific school zoning.

Is new construction or resale better when moving to DFW with kids?

Neither is automatically better; it's the levers again. New master-planned homes up north offer modern layouts and amenities but builder-drafted contracts and sometimes higher MUD or PID taxes. Resale in established suburbs offers mature neighborhoods and a different cost structure. Compare total long-term cost, not just incentives.

How do I narrow down DFW suburbs without visiting first?

Rank your four levers, set a realistic budget, and use the Suburb Matcher to turn those priorities into a top three before you fly. Then verify school zoning by address, test your true rush-hour commute, and tour only your shortlist in person; it saves weeks of guessing.
