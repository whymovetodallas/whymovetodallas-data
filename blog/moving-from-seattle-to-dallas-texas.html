Cost of Living

Seattle to Dallas: A Family Relocation Guide for Washington Movers

Kristen Carpentier · Licensed Texas Realtor®August 5, 2026 · 12 min read

Quick answer: Washington and Texas both skip the wage income tax today, so that is not where the money is. The real gap is housing, and it is large: a King County median around $889,000 against a Dallas-Fort Worth median near $400,000 in the same month. What offsets it is a higher Texas property tax rate, homeowners insurance that runs several times a typical Washington premium, and address level charges like municipal utility districts that most comparisons never mention. Compare one specific Seattle address against one specific DFW address, not the two states.

Seattle families reach out to me with the same two sentences almost every time. The house math looks unreal, and there has to be a catch. Both are true. The housing difference between the Puget Sound region and Dallas-Fort Worth is genuinely enormous, and there are three or four Texas costs that a Washington family has no reason to expect and will not find in a cost-of-living calculator.

A quick note on geography, because it changes every number below. "Dallas" usually means the city of Dallas itself. "Dallas-Fort Worth," or DFW, means the wider region, including Fort Worth and dozens of suburbs, each with its own tax rates and school district. Most relocating families land somewhere in DFW rather than in the city of Dallas, so this guide covers the region. I have labeled every figure with the geography and the period it actually describes, because mixing a statewide number with a metro number is how these comparisons go wrong.

The Tax Picture, Including the Part That Just Changed

The usual shorthand is that Washington and Texas are twins on tax: no state income tax in either one, so it is a wash. That was a fair summary a few years ago. It is not accurate now, and if you work in Seattle tech you probably already know why.

Wage income today. Neither state taxes ordinary wage income in 2026. For most households, that line genuinely does not change.

Capital gains. Washington taxes long-term capital gains at 7 percent above a standard deduction, and since tax year 2025 there is a second tier: gains above $1,000,000 are taxed at 9.9 percent. The standard deduction was $278,000 for tax year 2025 and is adjusted for inflation each year, so confirm the current figure with the Washington Department of Revenue before you plan around it. Texas has no state capital gains tax. If part of your move involves selling equity or a vesting event, this is not a rounding difference.

The part that is new. In March 2026, Washington enacted a 9.9 percent tax on Washington taxable income above a $1,000,000 standard deduction, applying to income received beginning January 1, 2028, with the first returns due in 2029. The Department of Revenue estimates it will reach roughly 21,000 taxpayers, and it is currently the subject of a constitutional challenge, so how it finally lands is not settled. I am not going to tell you this is why you should move; for the large majority of families it will never apply. But if you are weighing a long horizon and your compensation is equity heavy, it belongs in the picture rather than out of it.

The honest version: the income tax line is close to a wash for most families and always will be for wage earners. The tax difference that actually shows up in a Seattle family's budget is not income tax at all. It is property tax, and it runs the other direction.

What the Housing Gap Actually Is

This is the number that moves families, and it deserves to be measured properly rather than inflated.

On overall cost of living, the gap is real but moderate. The Bureau of Economic Analysis publishes Regional Price Parities, which measure the price level of a metro against the national average. For the 2024 data year, the Seattle-Tacoma-Bellevue metro came in at 111.1 and the Dallas-Fort Worth metro at 103.1. That is roughly 8 percent higher overall in Seattle, not the 30 or 40 percent that vendor calculators like to advertise.

On housing specifically, the gap is a different animal. The same BEA data puts the housing component at 151.3 for Seattle against 117.9 for DFW, about 28 percent higher. And when you look at actual sale prices rather than index values, it is wider still. In June 2026, the median sale price was about $889,000 in King County and about $400,000 across the Dallas-Fort Worth metro, per Northwest MLS and the Texas Real Estate Research Center respectively. So the headline is honest: on a like-for-like family home, a Seattle household really can free up several hundred thousand dollars of purchase price, or buy considerably more house and land for the same money. What it does not mean is that your monthly cost drops by the same proportion, because the carrying costs work differently here. That is the rest of this guide.

Texas Property Tax and the Homestead Exemption

Texas funds schools, cities, counties, and emergency services largely through local property tax. There is no single statewide rate. Your bill is the sum of the rates set by every taxing entity covering your specific parcel, applied to the appraised value after exemptions. Two rules matter most for a family arriving from Washington.

Sources: Texas Comptroller (Tax Code Sections 11.13(b) and 11.13(c)) and Texas Tax Code Section 23.23. Exemption amounts are set by the Legislature and can change. Confirm current figures with your county appraisal district.

The residence homestead exemption. Tax Code Section 11.13(b) requires school districts to exempt $140,000 of a qualifying residence homestead's value, raised from $100,000 by Proposition 13 in November 2025. Section 11.13(c) adds a further $60,000 school district exemption for owners age 65 or older or who qualify as disabled, raised from $10,000 by Proposition 11. Eligibility depends on owning and occupying the home as your principal residence, and you apply through the county appraisal district.

Filing does not happen at closing. I want to be direct about this because it is repeated as fact all over the internet. The exemption is an application you submit to the county appraisal district, and the timing rules depend on your ownership and occupancy dates. Nobody files it for you at the closing table. Confirm the process and the deadline with the appraisal district for your county.

The 10 percent appraisal cap, and when it starts. Texas limits the annual increase in the appraised value of a qualified residence homestead to 10 percent, plus the value of new improvements. The detail that catches buyers is the timing. Tax Code Section 23.23(c) provides that the limitation takes effect as of January 1 of the tax year following the first tax year the owner qualifies the property for a Section 11.13 exemption. In practice, your first year in the home is generally not protected by the cap. If you buy in a rising market, budget for that first reassessment.

The mistake I correct most often: using the seller's current tax figure as your estimate. Ask for the current assessed value, the current bill, the exemptions the seller holds, and then a fresh estimate based on your expected purchase price with no seller exemptions carried over. A retired seller with an over-65 exemption can be paying a fraction of what you will pay on the same house.

Insurance: The Line Washington Families Never See Coming

If there is one number I wish every Seattle family checked before they set a budget, it is this one. Washington is one of the least expensive states in the country for homeowners insurance. Texas is one of the most expensive, because of hail and severe storms.

For a same-year comparison, the National Association of Insurance Commissioners reported average annual homeowners premiums for 2021 of about $1,001 in Washington against $2,146 in Texas, with a national average of $1,411. More recently, the Texas Department of Insurance reports a Texas statewide average annual premium of $3,291 for 2024, up from $1,987 in 2020. Washington's insurance regulator does not publish a comparable statewide average, so I am not going to manufacture a 2024 Washington figure to sit beside it.

Read those two facts together and the direction is unmistakable: expect your homeowners premium to increase substantially, quite possibly to two or three times what you pay now. That is a real monthly number, and it eats into the housing savings.

Four things to ask about on any Texas quote:

  • The wind and hail deductible, and whether it is a flat dollar amount or a percentage of dwelling coverage. A percentage deductible on a $700,000 home is a much larger out-of-pocket number than most families expect.
  • Roof age and roof settlement terms. Replacement cost versus actual cash value on the roof changes both the premium and what you receive after a hailstorm, and North Texas gets hail.
  • Whether flood is covered. It generally is not. FEMA states that homeowners and renters insurance does not typically cover flood damage, and that more than 20 percent of flood claims come from properties outside high risk flood areas.
  • Claims history on the property, not just on you. Prior claims on the house itself can affect what an insurer will offer.

The DFW Cost Stack Most Comparisons Leave Out

State level comparisons stop at price, tax rate, and insurance. Three more DFW specific costs can move a monthly payment enough to change which house you buy, and none of them exists in the Puget Sound region in the same form.

Compare Your Two Addresses, Not the Two States

Here is the process I walk families through. It takes about an afternoon and it replaces every state average you have read, including the ones in this article.

  1. Price and financing. Set the target purchase price, down payment, and a current rate quote from a lender licensed in Texas. Ask the lender to escrow taxes at the reassessed value, not the seller's bill.
  2. The complete tax rate. Add every taxing entity on the parcel record, then check separately for a MUD or PID obligation. Do this for each candidate suburb; the spread across DFW is meaningful.
  3. Homestead eligibility and timing. Confirm with the county appraisal district when you can apply and which tax year the 10 percent cap first protects.
  4. A real insurance quote. Include the wind and hail deductible, roof settlement terms, the replacement cost limit, and a decision on separate flood coverage.
  5. Electricity and tolls. Use the actual home's usage history and the actual work and school routes, at the hours you will really drive them.
  6. The exact campus. Verify school assignment through the district's official address lookup for the specific property, then confirm it with the district before you make an offer.
  7. Capital gains timing. If a stock sale or vesting event is part of the plan, talk to your tax advisor about sequencing it against your Washington residency, because the timing can matter.

Run that stack for your current Seattle area home and for two or three DFW candidates. Whatever the answer turns out to be, it will be yours rather than a state average that describes nobody.

Work Location Is the First Filter

DFW has no single downtown that anchors everyone, which is the biggest structural difference from a Seattle commute. Major job centers are spread across Downtown Dallas, the Las Colinas and airport corridor in Irving, the Legacy area in Plano, Richardson, Frisco, and Downtown Fort Worth. Geography matters more than the company name, so here is where the big employers actually sit: Toyota North America and JPMorgan Chase in Plano, Charles Schwab in Westlake, State Farm in Richardson, American Airlines in Fort Worth, and AT&T, Southwest Airlines, and Texas Instruments in Dallas.

That spread is an advantage when you are job hunting and a trap when you are house hunting, because a community that is comfortable for an office in Plano can be punishing for a daily trip to Fort Worth. Map the actual office, the expected arrival time, and the number of in-office days before you look at a single listing. If both adults work, map both.

If you are fully remote, which describes a good share of the Seattle families I hear from, say it out loud and give yourself permission to choose on schools, price, and community instead of proximity. That freedom is exactly why so many remote tech families end up in the northern suburbs. Our guide to where to live in DFW based on where you work breaks the region down corridor by corridor.

The Weather Tradeoff

You are trading gray, mild, rainy winters for the opposite of that in both directions. DFW summers are hot, July and August especially, and the adjustment is real. In exchange you get far more sunshine year round and short, mild winters. Spring brings storms, and hail is common enough that it shapes the insurance market described above.

Most Pacific Northwest transplants tell me the sunshine is the part they underestimated in the good direction and the August heat is the part they underestimated in the other one. Practically, it changes your habits more than your happiness: outdoor time shifts to early morning and evening in the peak weeks, shade and a pool stop being luxuries, and your electricity bill has a summer shape instead of a winter one. Picture a real August before you commit, not an average annual temperature.

A Realistic Seattle to DFW Sequence

Seattle families often move on a 6 to 12 month horizon, especially remote workers choosing DFW deliberately rather than reacting to a transfer. Here is the rhythm that works.

About 90 days out

Confirm whether work anchors you anywhere, and whether the role is in-office, hybrid, or remote. Talk to a lender licensed in Texas and build the full monthly number, not just principal and interest. Decide whether you need to sell in Washington first, and whether a short term rental in DFW would take pressure off the timing. If a vesting date or a capital gains question is in play, loop in your tax advisor now rather than later.

About 60 days out

Narrow to two or three suburbs using work location, price band, and district. Run the cost stack above on a sample address in each, including the MUD and PID check. Plan a scouting trip or a set of virtual tours, and see each area on a weekday at the hours you would actually be driving.

About 30 days out

Tour homes, verify campus assignment on your top choices, get insurance quotes on the specific properties, and make an offer. During the option period, have the inspector walk you through roof condition, drainage, foundation movement, and HVAC capacity. Foundations and cooling systems behave differently on North Texas clay soil than on anything you are used to in the Puget Sound region.

Moving logistics

Interstate transit times vary by carrier, route, and season, so ask each mover for a written delivery window rather than assuming a number. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's Protect Your Move site explains how to verify a mover's registration and how valuation coverage works. Keep school and medical records with you, and plan for any stretch when the family may be down to one vehicle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do Washington and Texas both have no income tax?

Neither state taxes ordinary wage income in 2026, so for most households that line does not change. The differences are that Washington taxes long-term capital gains at 7 percent above a standard deduction and 9.9 percent above $1 million while Texas does not, and Washington enacted a 9.9 percent tax on income above $1 million beginning with 2028 income, which is currently under legal challenge. Texas has neither.

Is Dallas really that much cheaper than Seattle?

On housing, yes, substantially. In June 2026 the median sale price was about $889,000 in King County and about $400,000 across the Dallas-Fort Worth metro. On overall cost of living the gap is smaller, roughly 8 percent per BEA Regional Price Parities for 2024. The housing difference is the real story; the rest of the budget is closer than most comparisons suggest.

What is the catch when moving from Washington to Texas?

Three things. Texas property tax runs about 1.40 percent of value against roughly 0.75 percent in Washington. Homeowners insurance is far more expensive here, with a Texas statewide average of $3,291 for 2024. And many newer North DFW communities carry a MUD or PID assessment on top of ordinary rates. Summer heat is the fourth, and it is the one families adjust to fastest.

What is a MUD or PID, and why does it matter in DFW?

A municipal utility district or public improvement district is a local entity that adds an assessment on top of ordinary property taxes, commonly adding roughly 0.80 to 1.20 per $100 of value. It is most common in the newer master planned communities across North DFW, which is exactly where out of state families tend to look first. Check the property's district obligations and the seller's disclosure before you make an offer.

Do I file the Texas homestead exemption at closing?

No. The residence homestead exemption is an application you submit to your county appraisal district, and the timing depends on your ownership and occupancy dates. It is also worth knowing that the 10 percent annual appraisal cap generally takes effect the tax year after you first qualify, so your first year in the home is usually not protected. Confirm both with your county appraisal district.

Which DFW suburbs do tech families from Seattle usually start with?

McKinney, Allen, Plano, Frisco, and Prosper come up most often, for newer construction, master planned communities, and strong district ratings, with Celina and Little Elm as lower price band options. Remote workers can choose on schools and price; transferees should choose by commute first. Always verify the exact school campus for a specific home before buying.

The Bottom Line

A Seattle to Dallas-Fort Worth move is worth running the numbers on, and for most families the housing math holds up even after the offsets. Just run the right numbers. The income tax comparison is close to a wash and always has been. What actually decides your monthly cost is the complete local tax rate on one specific parcel, whether that parcel sits inside a MUD or PID, a real insurance quote with its wind and hail deductible, your electricity plan, your toll route, and the verified school campus. Get those six things for two or three specific homes and the decision makes itself.
