Moving to Dallas, Texas: The Ultimate Family Relocation Guide

Kristen Carpentier Licensed TX Realtor®  ·  June 3, 2026  ·  14 min read

If you're reading this, you've probably already decided that something needs to change — maybe a job offer landed, maybe the cost of living where you are has gotten out of hand, or maybe you've just spent one too many winters scraping ice off the car. Whatever brought you here, the question now is bigger and messier: what is it actually like to move a family to Dallas, and where do we even start?

I've helped hundreds of families make this exact move — from California, New York, Illinois, the Pacific Northwest, and from other parts of Texas. The thing almost no one tells you up front is that "moving to Dallas" rarely means moving to Dallas. It usually means choosing one suburb out of three dozen, each with its own school district, price point, commute, and personality. Get that choice right and the move feels like an upgrade in every direction. Get it wrong and you'll spend two years wishing you'd done more homework. This guide is the homework.

Why Families Are Moving to Dallas-Fort Worth

Dallas-Fort Worth is the fourth-largest metro in the country and one of the fastest-growing — the U.S. Census Bureau puts the metro at about 8.5 million people, growing by roughly 340 a day. That growth isn't an accident. The region pairs a deep, diversified job market with housing that still costs a fraction of what coastal families are used to, and it does it in a state with no income tax. For a family weighing a cross-country move, those three things — jobs, housing, taxes — are usually the whole ballgame.

But the families I work with rarely move for a spreadsheet. They move for what the spreadsheet buys: a four-bedroom house with a yard instead of a cramped condo, an A-rated public school down the street instead of a private-school tuition bill, weekend youth sports leagues, and a community of other families who showed up for the same reasons. DFW is, at its core, a place built around the suburban family — and that's exactly who it serves best.

If you want the fuller pros-and-cons picture before you go further, our guide on whether DFW is a good place to live walks through the honest version. But the short answer for most relocating parents is yes — with the caveat that the suburb you choose matters more than the metro you choose.

What It Actually Costs to Live Here

The single most important thing to understand about the cost of living in Dallas is that there is no single number. "Dallas" stretches from the city itself, where the median single-family home runs about $492K, out to a ring of suburbs where medians range from the high $300Ks to well over $1.3 million. Where you land in that range changes your monthly picture dramatically — far more than groceries, utilities, or anything else.

For a California family, those numbers are the headline of the entire move. A comparably sized, comparably newer home that runs well over $1 million in many Bay Area suburbs — or $800K–$900K across much of Los Angeles — often lands in Frisco or Allen for $500K–$680K, and that's before you account for the income-tax savings. For families coming from New York or Illinois, the housing gap is smaller but still meaningful, and the no-income-tax math adds real take-home pay on top. (Just remember that with 30-year mortgage rates in the mid-6% range in 2026, it's your monthly payment — not the sticker price alone — that determines what's comfortable.) I break the full numbers down in our cost of living in Dallas guide, but the pattern is consistent: housing is where DFW wins, and it wins decisively.

Don't skip this

The no-income-tax headline is real, but Texas makes some of it back through property taxes — often roughly 2%–2.6% of appraised value once you stack city, county, and school-district rates, depending on the exact address. The $140,000 school-district homestead exemption softens the blow for owner-occupants, but always ask for the total effective rate on a specific home before you fall in love with it.

Where to Live: Dallas Neighborhoods vs. Family Suburbs

This is the decision that makes or breaks the move. Broadly, you're choosing between two very different versions of DFW life: the walkable, characterful neighborhoods inside Dallas proper, or the master-planned, school-anchored suburbs to the north. Both are legitimate. They just serve different families.

Living in Dallas proper

If walkability, character, and a short commute to downtown matter more to you than a big yard, Dallas itself has genuinely great neighborhoods. Uptown is the young-professional and empty-nester core — high-rises, the Katy Trail, restaurants within walking distance. Lakewood wraps around White Rock Lake with historic homes, big trees, and a real neighborhood feel that families love. Highland Park and University Park (the "Park Cities") are the prestige address, with one of the best public school systems in the state — and price tags to match. Bishop Arts and Deep Ellum bring the arts, food, and music energy for buyers who want an urban lifestyle over a suburban one.

The catch for families is schools. Outside a few pockets like the Park Cities and parts of Lakewood, the public-school picture inside Dallas ISD is mixed, which is why many city-dwelling families end up paying for private school. If you're open to that — or your kids aren't school-age yet — Dallas proper can be a wonderful place to land. Browse what's available and see how the city's price points feel:

The family suburbs to the north

This is where the majority of relocating families with school-age kids end up, and for good reason. The corridor running north of Dallas — Plano, Frisco, Allen, McKinney, Prosper — and the cluster to the northwest — Flower Mound, Coppell, Southlake, Argyle — is built almost entirely around the suburban family. You get A and A+ school districts, newer and larger homes, master-planned amenities, youth sports on every corner, and streets full of other families who moved here for the same reasons you are.

The trade-off is that you give up walkability and an urban core for space and schools, and you'll be driving everywhere. For most parents, that's a trade they're happy to make. If you want the deep-dive comparison of the top options, start with our best DFW suburbs for families roundup, then dig into head-to-head matchups like Frisco vs. Plano and Frisco vs. Prosper.

Where the value is right now

The suburbs priced roughly $480K–$540K — McKinney, Allen, and parts of Plano — tend to offer the strongest combination of A-rated schools, newer construction, and reasonable commutes without paying the Frisco or Southlake premium. That's where I spend most of my time helping families land.

And don't overlook new construction. DFW has significant builder inventory at almost every price point, with move-in-ready homes in master-planned communities across Celina, Prosper, and Little Elm starting in the $400K–$500K range — brand-new schools and amenities included. You can browse active new-construction communities through my ShowingNew portal rather than chasing individual builder sites.

Schools: Why Most Families End Up in the Suburbs

For families with school-age kids, this is usually the deciding factor — not price, not commute, not square footage. The reason the northern suburbs command a premium is simple: their school districts are among the best in Texas. Frisco ISD, Allen ISD, Coppell ISD, and the Carroll ISD that serves most Southlake addresses routinely earn A or A+ ratings, and the families who move into those boundaries are, in large part, buying the schools.

Here's the single most important piece of advice in this entire guide: buy by school district boundary, not by city line. The two don't always match. A home that's technically in one city can feed into a different district's schools, and a street can split between two attendance zones. I've watched families fall in love with a house only to realize it zones to a school they didn't intend. Before you make an offer, confirm the exact elementary, middle, and high school the address feeds.

If schools are leading your search, our DFW school districts guide lays out the ratings side by side. It's the fastest way to narrow a long list of suburbs down to the three or four whose districts actually fit your kids.

Jobs and the DFW Economy

One reason DFW weathered the last few years better than most metros is that its economy doesn't lean on any single industry. The region runs on about 4.3 million nonfarm payroll jobs (per the Bureau of Labor Statistics) spread across corporate headquarters, finance, healthcare, technology, logistics, and aviation. That diversity matters most when one partner is relocating for a job and the other needs to find work after the move — there's simply a deeper pool of openings to land in.

The corporate roster is genuinely deep. Toyota North America, American Airlines, AT&T, Charles Schwab, JPMorgan Chase, McKesson, State Farm, and Fidelity all anchor major operations here, with a particularly dense concentration along the Legacy West / North Plano-Frisco corridor. If your job is in that corridor, suburbs like Plano, Frisco, and Allen put you within a reasonable commute. If you're still job-hunting, our roundup of the top employers in Dallas is a useful map of who's hiring and where they sit in the metro.

Getting Around: Cars, Tolls, and DART

Be honest with yourself about this one: DFW is a driving metro. DART — the light rail and bus system — covers Dallas proper and a handful of inner suburbs like Plano, Richardson, and Carrollton, and it works well if you live near a station and commute downtown. But most of the family suburbs have little to no transit, so plan on two cars for a typical household.

It helps to think about DFW in terms of commute corridors rather than individual cities — where you work narrows the suburb shortlist fast:

The flip side of all that driving is that the highway network is genuinely good, and toll roads make the long hauls faster — for a price. The Dallas North Tollway, President George Bush Turnpike (PGBT), and SH 121 can each run $80–$160 a month for a regular commuter depending on your route — the NTTA toll calculator will price your exact drive. Get a TollTag and build that line into your budget rather than being surprised by it. A 25-minute toll-road commute from Frisco to Legacy West is very doable; the same trip on free roads at rush hour is not.

The Honest Trade-offs

I'd be doing you a disservice if I only sold the upside. Every family I work with eventually runs into the same handful of realities, and it's far better to know them going in:

  • The summers are real. From June through September, triple-digit stretches are common — especially in July and August — and your electricity bill will spike to match. Many larger suburban homes see summer electric bills in the $250–$350+ range, depending on size, insulation, pool equipment, and thermostat habits. The flip side: mild winters, long springs and falls, and a pool culture that makes the heat manageable.
  • Property taxes sting. No income tax is wonderful; a 2%–2.6% combined property tax rate is the trade. For most relocating families the net still favors Texas, but run your specific numbers rather than assuming.
  • Hidden costs in new-build suburbs. Many newer communities in Celina, Prosper, and Little Elm sit inside MUD or PID districts that add 0.25%–0.75% to your effective tax rate to pay for the roads and amenities. It's not a gotcha if you know to ask.
  • You'll drive a lot. Add toll roads and hail insurance (a genuine North Texas line item) to the picture, and the "cheap Texas living" story gets a little more nuanced — though still very favorable for most.

If you want the unvarnished version, families often appreciate our piece on the mistakes people make moving to Dallas — most of them come down to underestimating exactly these trade-offs.

Your Move: A Realistic Timeline

Once you've settled on a target area, the logistics get much simpler. Here's the timeline I walk families through, whether they're 60 days out on a corporate transfer or planning a more relaxed six-month move:

DFW Relocation Checklist
  • Narrow to 2–3 target suburbs by school district and commute — not vibes alone
  • Get pre-approved with a lender so you know your real budget and effective tax impact
  • Plan a scouting trip (or a thorough virtual tour) of your shortlisted neighborhoods
  • Confirm the exact school attendance zones for any address before making an offer
  • Book movers early — 6–8 weeks out for peak summer, the busiest moving season here
  • Set up Texas essentials: electricity provider (deregulated market — you choose), TollTag, and a new-resident driver's license within 90 days
  • Enroll kids early; bring immunization records, proof of residency, and transcripts

The hiring-a-mover, packing, and moving-day basics are the same anywhere — get multiple quotes, label by room, keep an essentials box with you, and book well ahead in summer. The part that's specific to DFW, and the part worth getting right, is the suburb and school decision that everything else hangs on.

Should you rent first or buy right away?

It's the question I get from nearly every out-of-state family, and there's no universal answer. If your move is fast (a 60-day corporate transfer), your job location is certain, and you've done your school-district homework, buying right away saves you a double move and locks in your address before the next school year. If you're less sure where work or life will settle — or you simply want to feel out a few suburbs in person first — a 6–12 month rental is a smart, low-risk way to learn the metro before committing. The one thing I'd steer you away from is buying sight-unseen in a suburb you've never driven through. A scouting trip or a thorough virtual tour comes first, every time.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Dallas a good place to move to for families?

Yes — Dallas-Fort Worth is consistently one of the top U.S. metros for family relocation, and the reasons are concrete: roughly 4.3 million jobs across diversified industries, no state income tax, A-rated suburban school districts, and housing that costs a fraction of what coastal families are used to. The honest trade-offs are car dependency, hot summers, and higher property taxes. For most families I work with, the math and the lifestyle both come out in DFW's favor — as long as you choose the right suburb.

How much money do you need to move to Dallas?

For a comfortable suburban family lifestyle with a home in the $450K–$520K range, two cars, and activities for kids, a household income of $110,000–$140,000 is a reasonable target — though comfort always depends on your down payment, debt, and current mortgage rates. For the premium districts of Frisco and Flower Mound, budget $150,000–$180,000 to feel comfortable rather than stretched, with Southlake's luxury market a notch higher again. If you're renting first while you learn the metro, $80,000–$95,000 works in most suburbs. Plan for upfront moving costs of $3,000–$8,000 for a long-distance move plus a down payment and closing costs once you buy.

What are the best suburbs of Dallas for families?

The standout family suburbs north of Dallas are Frisco, Plano, Allen, McKinney, Flower Mound, Prosper, Coppell, Southlake, Argyle, and Colleyville — all anchored by A or A+ school districts. Frisco and Southlake sit at the premium end ($680K and $1.3M median). Allen and McKinney offer the best balance of A-rated schools and value in the $500K range. The right one depends on your budget, your commute corridor, and whether you want established neighborhoods or brand-new master-planned construction.

Is it cheaper to live in Dallas than California?

Significantly. DFW housing runs roughly 55%–65% below the Bay Area and 40%–50% below Los Angeles for comparable homes in comparable school districts — a $1.2 million Bay Area house often has a near-equivalent in Frisco or Allen for $500K–$680K. Texas also has no state income tax versus California's 9.3%+ marginal rate for most professional households. The offsets are higher property taxes and the cost of running A/C through a Texas summer, but for nearly every California family I help, the overall difference is dramatic, not marginal.

What is a good salary to live comfortably in Dallas, Texas?

In the suburbs, a household income of $110,000–$140,000 supports a comfortable family lifestyle with a home in the $450K–$520K range. Inside Dallas proper, where the median is around $492K but rentals and condos run lower, $90,000–$120,000 is workable depending on neighborhood. For the top-tier districts like Frisco and Flower Mound, aim for $150,000+, and treat Southlake as a separate luxury-market conversation. Because there's no state income tax, a given salary stretches noticeably further here than in California, New York, or Illinois.

Do you need a car to live in Dallas?

For almost all families, yes. DART light rail and bus service covers parts of Dallas proper and a handful of inner suburbs, but the metro is built around highways and most family suburbs have little to no transit. If you're living in Uptown or near a DART station and working downtown, you can manage with one car or none. In Frisco, Prosper, or Flower Mound, plan on two cars and factor toll roads (the Dallas North Tollway, PGBT, and SH 121) into your monthly budget.

Should families live in Dallas proper or the suburbs?

It comes down to what you're optimizing for. Dallas-proper neighborhoods like Lakewood, Highland Park, and Uptown offer walkability, character, and a shorter commute to downtown, but the public-school picture is mixed outside a few pockets. The northern suburbs trade urban energy for A-rated schools, newer and larger homes, master-planned amenities, and safer, quieter streets. Most relocating families with school-age kids end up in the suburbs for the schools and the space — but if walkability and an urban lifestyle matter more to you, Dallas proper is worth a serious look.

Should you rent or buy when relocating to Dallas?

If your job location is certain and your timeline is tight, buying right away avoids a double move and locks in your school district before the next school year. If you're unsure where you'll settle or want to experience a few suburbs in person, a 6–12 month rental is a low-risk way to learn the metro before committing. Either way, don't buy in a suburb you've never driven through — a scouting trip or thorough virtual tour should come first.

What should I know before moving to Dallas?

Five things surprise newcomers most: (1) property taxes are high (roughly 2%–2.6% combined in Dallas County) even though there's no income tax; (2) summers are genuinely hot — 100°F+ stretches from June through September; (3) you'll likely need two cars and a toll tag; (4) school district lines, not city lines, should drive where you buy; and (5) new-construction suburbs can carry MUD or PID surcharges that add 0.25%–0.75% to your effective tax rate. Knowing these going in keeps your budget and expectations realistic.
