Moving to DFW

Moving from California to DFW: What Your Money Actually Buys

Kristen Carpentier Licensed TX Realtor®  ·  June 4, 2026  ·  15 min read

More of the families I help relocate come from California than anywhere else, and the conversation almost always starts the same way: "We love a lot about California, but we cannot make the math work for our kids anymore." If that sounds familiar, this guide is for you. I am not here to talk you out of California or sell you a fantasy about Texas. I am here to show you — in real numbers — what your California budget actually buys in Dallas-Fort Worth, where the savings come from, what the genuine trade-offs are, and which suburb may actually be the right fit for your specific family. I have helped families from the Bay Area, Orange County, Los Angeles, and Sacramento navigate this exact decision. What I can tell you is that the right answer is never just "move to Texas." It is always "let's figure out which part of Texas actually fits your family."

Quick answer: For a professional household earning around $150,000 and buying in the $400,000–$550,000 range in DFW, the combination of no state income tax and lower housing costs commonly produces $25,000–$40,000 in annual savings — with the four-bedroom you are stretching to afford in California often becoming a newer, larger home in a top-ranked DFW school district at a meaningfully lower monthly payment.

Why California Families Keep Landing in DFW

The overall cost of living in Texas runs roughly 30–40 percent lower than California, driven mostly by dramatically cheaper housing and the absence of a state income tax. For families, that gap usually shows up in three places: the mortgage payment, the paycheck after taxes, and the size and quality of the home you can actually afford. Dallas-Fort Worth in particular draws California families because it pairs that affordability with a deep set of highly rated suburban school districts to the north — which is exactly what parents are trying to protect when they leave the coast.

DFW also has the job diversity to absorb relocating Californians at almost every level. Toyota's North American headquarters is in Plano. JPMorgan has a massive campus in Plano. Charles Schwab relocated to Westlake. American Airlines and Southwest are both based here. The tech and finance corridor that runs along the Tollway is real, and it is part of why Bay Area families in particular feel like DFW is a familiar professional ecosystem in an unfamiliar landscape.

The Real Savings Math: A Transparent Breakdown

The "$25,000–$40,000 in annual savings" claim shows up in a lot of California-to-Texas content, and most of the time it is not explained. Here is what the math actually looks like for a family earning $150,000 per year — buying a $900,000 home in California versus a comparable $450,000 home in DFW (both with 20% down, 30-year fixed at 6.8%).

Category California DFW Est. Annual Difference
State income tax (on $150K HHI) ~$8,500/yr $0 Save ~$8,500
Monthly mortgage P&I ($720K vs $360K loan) ~$4,700/mo ~$2,350/mo Save ~$28,200/yr
Annual property taxes ~$6,750 (0.75% on $900K) ~$7,650 (1.7% on $450K) Pay ~$900 more
Homeowners insurance ~$2,000/yr ~$2,500/yr Pay ~$500 more
Net estimated annual advantage ~$35,300/yr

Assumes 20% down, 30-year fixed at 6.8%. California income tax estimate for married filing jointly at $150K; actual tax depends on taxable income, filing status, deductions, and credits — consult a CPA. Property tax estimates are approximate. Figures vary by specific location and individual circumstances.

The Texas property tax offset is real but modest compared to the mortgage and income tax savings. The homestead exemption on the Texas side (which reduces taxable value on the school district portion by $140,000, per TX Tax Code §11.13(b)) helps close the gap further for families who file promptly after closing. I tell every California client the same thing: the property tax is the trade-off, not the dealbreaker. Run the full picture.

The Housing Math: What Your Money Buys

This is the number that makes families sit up. Median Dallas-area family homes in top suburbs run roughly $400,000–$600,000, while the equivalent in Los Angeles or Orange County often lands at $900,000 to well over $1 million. In practice, that means the four-bedroom you are stretching to afford in California is often a newer, larger five-bedroom with a backyard, a two-car garage, and a neighborhood pool in a strong DFW school district — at a meaningfully lower monthly payment.

In Frisco, that same budget bought them a 4-bedroom with a backyard, a 3-car garage, and their kids in one of the top school districts in Texas. No State Income Tax: What It Means for Your Paycheck

Texas has no state income tax — none. That matters significantly for professional households, but it is worth being precise about how it works: the benefit depends on your taxable income, filing status, deductions, and credits — not just your gross salary. For many professional California households earning $120,000–$200,000, California state income tax commonly runs $7,000–$15,000 or more per year. In Texas, that number is $0. Every single year.

California has a progressive state income tax with a top marginal rate of 13.3%. For a married household at $150,000, effective state income tax is roughly $8,000–$9,000 annually depending on deductions. In Texas: $0. That difference compounds every year you live here.

Here is how the income tax difference looks at a $120,000 household income for context:

  • California (depends on taxable income and filing status): roughly $6,500–$9,000 in state income tax annually for a typical professional household at this income level
  • Texas: $0
  • Monthly difference: $540–$750 more take-home pay every month

For higher earners — particularly Bay Area tech workers and Los Angeles professionals with stock compensation — the savings are even more pronounced. The 9.3% California marginal rate that applies above roughly $136,000 in taxable income (married filing jointly, 2025) is exactly why executives and senior engineers disproportionately drive the California-to-DFW migration. California also charges an additional State Disability Insurance (SDI) contribution of 1.1% on all wages — a line item that disappears on your first Texas paycheck.

The Honest Trade-Off: Property Taxes by Suburb

Texas funds its budget partly through higher property taxes, so the rate is meaningfully higher than California's. Average property tax rates run about 0.75 percent in California versus roughly 1.7–2.2 percent across DFW suburbs. On a $450,000 home in DFW, that is roughly $7,650–$9,900 per year versus $3,375 in California on the same assessed value. That is a real difference — and I tell every client to factor it in from day one.

Here is a county-level breakdown to help you plan more precisely, because rates vary meaningfully by where you land:

The Texas Homestead Exemption

Texas offers a $140,000 homestead exemption off the school district portion of your appraised value (TX Tax Code §11.13(b)). On a $500,000 home, that reduces the school district's taxable value to $360,000 — meaningfully lowering your effective school tax. You must apply for the exemption through the county appraisal district where the property is located, typically between January 1 and April 30 in the year following your purchase. It does not apply automatically — you have to file. I remind every buyer I work with to do this in their first spring in Texas.

The rule of thumb still holds: if you are buying under $600,000 in DFW, you almost always come out clearly ahead on the full tax picture. If you are buying above $700,000, sit down with a CPA who knows both California and Texas tax before you list your California home. There are no surprises when you plan — and plenty of them when you do not.

How Much Does It Cost to Move from California to DFW?

Most California families I talk to know what their California savings will look like once they are here. What they often underestimate is the actual cost to get here. Here is a realistic range for a 3–4 bedroom household move from Southern California or the Bay Area to DFW:

Move Type Estimated Cost Notes
Full-service movers (pack & move) $5,000–$12,000+ Varies by home size, origin city, and season
PODS / container (self-pack) $3,500–$7,000 Lower cost, more flexibility on timing
Rental truck (drive yourself) $1,500–$3,000 + fuel Fuel from LA to DFW adds ~$400–$600 for a large truck
Car shipping (per vehicle) $800–$1,400 Open transport; enclosed adds ~$400–$700
Temporary housing (if needed) $3,000–$8,000/mo Furnished apartments or extended-stay hotels in DFW suburbs
Timing Is Everything

Summer moves (June–August) carry a 20–30% premium — every family trying to close before school starts is competing for the same trucks. If you have flexibility, early May or late September saves real money. Also factor in: Texas school enrollment deadlines, lease end dates if you are renting first, and your California closing timeline. Most families underestimate how long the whole sequence takes. I encourage clients to start the DFW housing search at least 90 days before they need to be in a home — earlier if school start dates are driving the timeline.

On Schools — A Word of Caution

Southlake's Carroll ISD is consistently one of the highest-rated school districts in Texas by major ranking sources — that reputation is real. But "best ISD" is not always the right question. The right question is which campus your child will actually attend, and whether it fits your kid's specific academic style, size, and program preferences. I have seen families choose Southlake for the Carroll ISD name and discover their child would have thrived just as well — and possibly more — at Frisco ISD or Prosper ISD. Visit specific campuses, not just district websites, before you buy a home.

What California Families Usually Miss

I believe in going into this move clear-eyed. The families who regret relocating to Texas are almost always the ones who were not honest with themselves about what they were leaving behind. So here is my honest list of what California genuinely has that DFW does not — and how families tend to adapt:

  • Ocean access. Galveston is the nearest Gulf Coast beach — about 4.5 hours from most DFW suburbs. It is not the Pacific. Families who love a weekly beach day have to recalibrate. Most adapt by leaning into DFW's lake lifestyle: Grapevine Lake, Lake Lewisville, Lake Ray Hubbard, and Possum Kingdom are all within reach.
  • Mountains and skiing. The nearest skiing from DFW is Ruidoso, New Mexico — about 6 hours. Taos is 8. Families who ski regularly tend to plan one or two annual ski trips instead of weekend runs.
  • Mild summer weather. This is the one most California families underestimate. July and August in DFW regularly hit 100°F or higher. Outdoor comfort in summer requires real planning — morning activities, indoor facilities, and pools. The flip side: DFW winters are shorter and milder than most of the country, and spring and fall are beautiful.
  • Walkability. DFW is car country. Most suburbs have a Walk Score under 30. If you come from a walkable California neighborhood and value being able to walk to coffee or the farmers market, you will feel this shift. Some walkable pockets exist — downtown McKinney, Southlake Town Square, Legacy West in Plano — but they are the exception.
  • Established social circles. Most California families I have worked with say this was the hardest part. Starting over socially as an adult is genuinely difficult, and it takes time. Youth sports leagues, school parent groups, neighborhood events, and church communities are the fastest on-ramps in DFW — and they are genuinely active here.
  • Cultural and political environment. Texas leans conservative, and DFW suburbs lean more so. Families coming from coastal California metros often feel this cultural shift in daily life. This is something to think about honestly, not something to dismiss or overstate.

None of these trade-offs are dealbreakers for most California families — but all of them are real. The families who thrive in DFW are the ones who moved toward what Texas offers, not just away from California's cost. For a candid look at the full adjustment, our post on why some Californians regret moving to Texas is worth reading before you commit.

DFW Cost Surprises California Families Don't Expect

Every California family I work with has thought about property taxes and knows housing is cheaper. What they often do not plan for are the DFW-specific costs that reliably catch newcomers off guard:

  • Wind and hail insurance deductibles. Most DFW homeowner's policies have a 1%–2% wind/hail deductible — separate from your standard deductible. On a $500,000 home, that is a $5,000–$10,000 out-of-pocket exposure per hail event. North Texas averages 8–10 significant hail events per year in some areas. Budget for it, and understand your deductible before you close.
  • Toll roads. Texas has an extensive toll system. Families commuting regularly on the Dallas North Tollway, President George Bush Turnpike, or SH 121 can spend $100–$250/month in tolls. TxTag makes it manageable, but it is a real line item California transplants do not always anticipate.
  • Summer electric bills. Running central AC around the clock in August in a 2,500–3,500 sq ft home costs $250–$400+ per month, sometimes more. This shocks families from coastal California who rarely run AC heavily. Plan for it in your monthly budget.
  • MUD and PID taxes. Municipal Utility District (MUD) and Public Improvement District (PID) assessments are common in newer outer-ring suburbs like Celina, Prosper, Little Elm, and Anna. These add 0.25%–0.75% to the effective property tax rate and do not always appear in the headline tax rate. Always ask your agent or attorney about MUD/PID status before making an offer in a new community.
  • Pest control and lawn care. Texas has fire ants, scorpions, mosquitoes, and summers brutal enough to require irrigation and professional lawn care. Budget $400–$600/year for pest control and $1,500–$4,000/year for lawn maintenance, depending on lot size.
  • Youth sports costs. DFW has exceptional competitive youth sports — but "competitive" means expensive. Club soccer, lacrosse, basketball, and baseball programs can run $2,000–$8,000+ per season per child. Recreational leagues are far more affordable, and they exist in abundance.

California to DFW Family Moving Checklist

One section that is consistently missing from California-to-Texas relocation content is the practical sequence — what to actually do, and when. Here is a condensed checklist I give to the families I work with:

6–12 Months Before You Move

  • Research DFW school districts by campus — not just ISD rating. The specific elementary, middle, and high school feeder pattern matters as much as the district name.
  • Consult with a DFW relocation specialist about which suburbs match your commute, budget, and lifestyle priorities. A bad suburb decision is expensive to fix.
  • Get pre-approved with a lender who builds Texas property taxes into the monthly payment calculation — many California lenders underestimate this and it creates sticker shock at closing.
  • Plan a DFW scouting trip — at least 3 days, ideally 5. Drive different suburbs during morning commute hours. Visit school campuses. Walk the neighborhoods you're considering.
  • Compare your California equity to DFW price points across multiple suburbs. Knowing your exact budget range in DFW before you visit saves enormous time.

2–6 Months Before You Move

  • Get moving quotes from at least 3 companies — request dates outside peak summer season if possible (June–August is 20–30% more expensive).
  • Decide: rent first or buy? If school enrollment is driving timing and you know your target district, buying directly often makes more sense. If you need to learn the area, rent in your target suburb for 6–12 months.
  • Reserve temporary housing or an extended-stay option in your target suburb — especially important if your California close and DFW close do not overlap cleanly.
  • Research Texas vehicle registration and driver's license requirements (new Texas residents have 30 and 90 days respectively after establishing domicile).

First 90 Days After You Arrive

  • File for Texas homestead exemption — between January 1 and April 30, through the county appraisal district where your home is located. This is one of the most commonly missed steps. Do not miss it in your first spring.
  • Update your Texas driver's license (within 90 days of establishing residency).
  • Update vehicle registration (within 30 days).
  • Update homeowners and auto insurance — Texas hail deductibles are different from California. Make sure your agent explains the wind/hail clause.
  • Set up utilities — and ask about budget billing for electric to smooth out the August spikes.
  • Enroll kids in school — bring immunization records; Texas requirements differ slightly from California.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money will my family actually save moving from California to Dallas?

For a household earning around $150,000 and buying in the $400,000–$500,000 range in DFW, the combination of no state income tax and lower housing commonly produces $25,000–$40,000 in annual savings. Higher earners save more from the tax side. The housing savings are largest when buying under $600,000 in DFW. Every situation is different, and the exact number depends on your income, which California metro you leave, and which DFW suburb you choose.

How much does it cost to move from California to DFW?

For a 3–4 bedroom home, full-service movers from California to DFW typically run $5,000–$12,000 depending on home size, origin city, and time of year. PODS or container services run $3,500–$7,000. Renting a truck and driving yourself costs $1,500–$3,000 plus fuel. Car shipping adds $800–$1,400 per vehicle. Summer moves (June–August) carry a 20–30% premium — if you can plan around the school year, early May or late September is cheaper.

Are Texas property taxes going to wipe out my savings?

Usually not. Texas property tax rates are higher — roughly 1.7–2.0 percent depending on suburb, versus about 0.75 percent in California — but because DFW home prices are significantly lower and there is no state income tax, most families still come out clearly ahead, especially when buying under $600,000. Texas also offers a $140,000 homestead exemption off the school district portion of your appraised value. Families buying above $700,000 in DFW should run the full numbers with a CPA before listing their California home.

What does a California family home budget buy in DFW?

A budget that buys a stretched four-bedroom in much of California often buys a newer, larger home with a backyard and a two-car garage in a strong DFW school district, at a meaningfully lower monthly payment. Median family homes in top DFW suburbs run roughly $400,000–$600,000, compared with $850,000 or more for similar homes in most California metros.

Which DFW suburbs are best for families moving from California?

Frisco and Plano are popular with Bay Area tech families for the schools and job corridor. Prosper, Flower Mound, and McKinney suit families wanting space and newer construction. Southlake and Argyle appeal to those wanting a smaller-town feel with a top-rated district. The right suburb depends on your budget, commute, airport access needs, and your kids' school situation.

Do California families regret moving to Texas?

Some do — and being honest about the trade-offs is how to avoid that outcome. The families who struggle most are the ones who moved purely on financial logic without visiting DFW first, or who chose the wrong suburb for their lifestyle. The families who thrive are the ones who came clear-eyed: they knew summers would be hot, that the beach was far away, and that community takes time to build. The financial savings are real, but so is the adjustment. Read our honest guide to why some Californians regret moving to Texas before you commit.

Is DFW better for families than Austin?

It depends on your priorities. Austin has a more walkable urban feel and is closer to outdoor amenities like hiking and state parks. DFW is significantly larger with more corporate job diversity, more suburb choices at every price point, and generally lower home prices for comparable school quality. DFW also has two major international airports (DFW and Love Field). Families who value suburban school districts and space per dollar consistently find DFW the stronger fit — especially at the $400,000–$700,000 price point.

What should California parents know about DFW school districts?

In DFW, the ISD rating matters, but the specific campus your child will actually attend matters just as much. Research both the district and the elementary, middle, and high school feeder pattern before you buy a home. Buying one block outside a boundary can mean a different school even in the same neighborhood. Always map the school zones before falling in love with a house. Our DFW school districts guide and our guide to the best DFW school districts for families are good starting points.

Should we rent first or buy when we move to DFW?

Both strategies work. Renting first gives you time to learn the area without locking in before you know which suburb actually fits your family. Buying first locks in a rate and a neighborhood, which is useful if school enrollment is urgent or the market is competitive. If you have school-age kids starting in the fall and know which district you want, buying is often the better move. If you have flexibility, renting in your target area for 6–12 months first can prevent costly mistakes. I help families think through this decision as part of every relocation consultation.

What salary do you need to live comfortably in DFW with kids?

Comfortable family life in DFW with one or two kids — in a 4-bedroom home in a good school district — typically starts around $100,000 household income. A $150,000 household income in DFW typically provides a quality of life that would require $200,000–$250,000 or more to match in most California metros. Most California families relocating for budget reasons find that their California salary goes significantly further in DFW — and that is before accounting for the elimination of state income tax.
