Moving from Florida to Dallas: An Honest Cost, Tax, and Suburb Guide for Families

Kristen Carpentier · Licensed Texas Realtor®August 2, 2026 · 11 min read

Quick answer: Neither Florida nor Texas charges a state individual income tax, so the real comparison is the monthly cost of owning one specific home in one specific place. Both states carry high insurance exposure, and Texas property tax is local and resets around what you pay for the house. Compare your actual Florida address against a specific Dallas-Fort Worth address using the tax rate, insurance quote, flood decision, electricity, and toll assumptions attached to each one.

Most families who contact me from Florida start with the same sentence: everything looks cheaper in Texas. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the savings are much smaller than the listing prices suggest, and once in a while the monthly number goes the wrong way. The difference is almost never the state. It is the address.

A quick note on geography before we start, because it matters for every number below. "Dallas" usually means the city of Dallas itself. "Dallas-Fort Worth" (DFW) means the wider region, including Fort Worth and dozens of suburbs, each with its own tax rates and school district. Most relocating families end up somewhere in DFW rather than in the city of Dallas, so this guide covers the region. Texas statewide averages are useful background only, and I have labeled every figure with the geography and period it actually describes.

If you want the wider picture first, start with the DFW Family Relocation Guide, then use the Suburb Matcher once you are ready to narrow the map.

What Actually Changes When You Leave Florida

Florida and Texas have real similarities. Both are large, fast growing Sun Belt states. Neither imposes an individual state income tax, so for most households that line does not change at all in a Florida to Texas move. The Census Bureau publishes annual state-to-state migration flows if you want to see how the exchange between the two states is trending in a given year.

What does change is the shape of your housing cost. In Florida, your recurring pain is usually the insurance premium, and your property tax has likely been held down for years by a homestead assessment limitation. In Texas, insurance is also expensive, and property tax is high and reassessed around what you pay for the home. That combination is why a state-level "cheaper" claim tells you almost nothing useful.

The honest version: a Florida to DFW move can free up real money, especially on price per square foot and on what a given budget buys. It is not an automatic discount on the monthly cost of ownership. Two homes at the same price in two different DFW suburbs can carry monthly costs that differ by hundreds of dollars.

Insurance: The Assumption That Costs Florida Families the Most

The most common thing I have to correct is the belief that homeowners insurance becomes a small line item after a move to Texas. It does not.

The Texas Department of Insurance reports a statewide average annual homeowners premium of $3,291 for 2024, up from $1,987 in 2020, in its Texas homeowners insurance market overview. That is a statewide average across all Texas homes and policy forms, not a quote for your house. Research from the Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas published in April 2026 found the median Texas homeowner paid about 60 percent more for home insurance in 2024 than in 2019, against roughly 30 percent nationally, and identified Dallas-Fort Worth as one of the two Texas metros carrying the heaviest insurance burden.

So the useful comparison is not Florida average versus Texas average. It is your current declarations page against a real quote on a real DFW address. Florida's Office of Insurance Regulation publishes an official CHOICES homeowners rate comparison tool for Florida properties, and any Texas agent can quote a specific DFW address once you have one in mind. Get both numbers before you set a purchase price.

Four things to ask about on any Texas quote:

  • The wind and hail deductible, and whether it is a flat dollar amount or a percentage of the dwelling coverage. A percentage deductible on a $600,000 home is a much larger out-of-pocket number than most families expect.
  • Roof age and roof settlement terms, because replacement cost versus actual cash value on the roof can change both the premium and what you receive after a storm.
  • Whether flood is covered. It generally is not. FEMA states plainly that homeowners and renters insurance does not typically cover flood damage, and that more than 20 percent of flood claims come from properties outside high risk flood areas. Inland DFW is not hurricane exposed, but it is not flood free, and flood coverage is a separate policy decision here just as it was in Florida.
  • Claims history on the property, not just on you. Prior claims on the house can affect what an insurer will offer.

How Texas Property Tax and the Homestead Exemption Work

Texas funds schools, cities, counties, and emergency services largely through local property tax. There is no single statewide rate. Your bill is the sum of the rates set by every taxing entity that covers your specific parcel, applied to your appraised value after exemptions.

Two rules matter most for a family arriving from Florida:

$0Individual state income tax, in both Texas and Florida
$140,000School district residence homestead exemption in Texas
10%Annual appraisal cap, starting the year after you qualify

Sources: Texas Comptroller (Tax Code Sections 11.13(b) and 11.13(c)) and Texas Tax Code Section 23.23. Exemption amounts and rules are set by the Legislature and can change. Confirm the current figures with your county appraisal district.

The residence homestead exemption. Texas Tax Code Section 11.13(b) requires school districts to provide a $140,000 exemption on a qualifying residence homestead, and Section 11.13(c) requires an additional $60,000 school district exemption for owners who are age 65 or older or who qualify as disabled, according to the Texas Comptroller's residence homestead guidance. Eligibility depends on owning and occupying the home as your principal residence, and you apply through the county appraisal district. Filing is not something that happens automatically at the closing table, and the timing rules depend on your ownership and occupancy dates, so confirm the process with the appraisal district for your county.

The 10 percent appraisal limitation. Texas caps the annual increase in the appraised value of a qualified residence homestead at 10 percent, plus the value of new improvements. The important detail for a buyer is when it starts. Texas Tax Code Section 23.23(c) provides that the limitation "takes effect as of January 1 of the tax year following the first tax year the owner qualifies the property for an exemption under Section 11.13." In practice, your first year in the home is generally not protected by the cap.

Why this hits Florida sellers hardest: if you have owned your Florida home for years, its assessed value has probably been held below market by Florida's Save Our Homes assessment limitation, described by the Florida Department of Revenue. Your current Florida tax bill reflects years of that protection. A new Texas purchase starts fresh at your purchase price. Comparing your old, capped Florida bill to a new, uncapped Texas bill is the single most common budgeting mistake I see.

One more rule with no exceptions: never use the seller's current tax figure as your estimate. Ask for the current assessed value, the current bill, the exemptions the seller holds, and a fresh estimate based on your expected purchase price with no seller exemptions carried over.

Compare Your Two Addresses, Not the Two States

Here is the process I walk families through. It takes about an afternoon and it replaces every state average you have read.

  1. Price and financing. Set the target purchase price, down payment, and a current rate quote from a lender licensed in Texas. Ask the lender to escrow taxes at the reassessed value, not the seller's bill.
  2. The complete tax rate. Add every taxing entity on the parcel record, then check for a MUD or PID obligation. Do this for each candidate suburb, because the spread across DFW is meaningful.
  3. Homestead eligibility and timing. Confirm with the county appraisal district when you can apply and which tax year the 10 percent cap first protects.
  4. A real insurance quote. Include the wind and hail deductible, the roof settlement terms, the replacement cost limit, and a decision on separate flood coverage.
  5. Electricity and tolls. Use the actual home's usage history and the actual work and school routes, at the hours you will really drive them.
  6. The exact campus. Verify school assignment through the district's official address lookup for the specific property, then confirm it with the district before you make an offer.

Run that stack for your current Florida home and for two or three DFW candidates. Whatever the answer turns out to be, it will be yours rather than a statewide average that describes no one.

Work Location Is the First Filter

DFW has no single downtown that anchors everyone. Major job centers are spread across Downtown Dallas, Las Colinas and the airport corridor in Irving, the Legacy area in Plano, Richardson, Frisco, and Downtown Fort Worth. The Dallas Regional Chamber counts dozens of Fortune 1000 headquarters across the region, including American Airlines in Fort Worth, Charles Schwab in Westlake, McKesson in Irving, and AT&T, Southwest Airlines, and Texas Instruments in Dallas.

That density is an advantage when you are job hunting and a trap when you are house hunting, because a community that is comfortable for an office in Plano can be punishing for a daily trip to Fort Worth. Map the actual office, the expected arrival time, and the number of in-office days before you look at a single listing. If both adults work, map both. If you are remote, say so out loud and give yourself permission to choose on schools and community instead.

A Realistic Florida to DFW Sequence

About 90 days out

Confirm where work will anchor you and whether the role is in-office, hybrid, or remote. Talk to a lender licensed in Texas and build the full monthly number, not just principal and interest. Decide whether you need to sell in Florida first, and whether a short term rental in DFW would take pressure off the timing.

About 60 days out

Narrow to two or three suburbs using work location, price band, and district. Run the cost stack above on a sample address in each. Plan a scouting trip or a set of virtual tours, and see each area on a weekday at the hours you would actually be driving.

About 30 days out

Tour homes, verify campus assignment on your top choices, get insurance quotes on the specific properties, and make an offer. During the option period, have the inspector walk you through roof condition, drainage, foundation movement, and HVAC performance, all of which behave differently here than in coastal Florida construction.

Moving logistics

Interstate transit times vary by carrier, route, and season, so ask each mover for its written delivery window rather than assuming a number. The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration's Protect Your Move site explains how to verify a mover's registration and how valuation coverage works. Keep school and medical records with you, and plan for any stretch when the family may be down to one vehicle.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is Texas cheaper than Florida for families?

It depends entirely on the two addresses. Neither state charges an individual income tax, and both carry high insurance costs. Texas property tax is higher and reassesses at your purchase price, while your Florida bill has likely been held down by the Save Our Homes limitation. Compare your actual current costs against a specific DFW property.

Do both Florida and Texas have no state income tax?

Neither state imposes an individual state income tax, so that line is generally a wash in a Florida to Texas move. The meaningful differences are property tax, which is local and higher in Texas, and homeowners insurance, which is expensive in both states for different reasons. Ask a tax professional about your specific situation.

Will my homeowners insurance go down when I move to Dallas?

Do not assume it will. The Texas Department of Insurance reports a statewide average annual homeowners premium of $3,291 for 2024, and Dallas Fed research identifies Dallas-Fort Worth as carrying one of the heaviest insurance burdens in Texas. DFW has no hurricane exposure, but hail is a major peril here. Get a real quote before setting your budget.

Does Texas homeowners insurance cover flooding?

Generally no. FEMA states that homeowners and renters insurance does not typically cover flood damage, and that more than 20 percent of flood claims come from properties outside high risk flood areas. Inland DFW is not hurricane exposed, but flood coverage is still a separate decision. Check the flood map for the specific property.

What is a MUD or PID, and why does it matter in DFW?

A municipal utility district or public improvement district is a local entity that adds an assessment on top of ordinary property taxes, often in newer master planned communities across North DFW. It can add a meaningful amount to a monthly payment. Check the property's district obligations and the seller's disclosure before you make an offer.

Which DFW suburbs do families relocating from Florida usually start with?

Plano, McKinney, Allen, Frisco, and Prosper come up most often because of newer construction, master planned communities, and strong districts, with Celina and Little Elm as lower price band options. Choose by work commute first, then verify the exact school zoning for any specific home before you buy.

The Bottom Line

A Florida to Dallas-Fort Worth move is worth running the numbers on. Just run the right numbers. The state comparison is mostly noise once you know that neither state taxes income and both charge a lot for insurance. The decision lives at the address: the complete tax rate, the MUD or PID obligation, the insurance quote and its wind and hail deductible, the flood decision, the electricity plan, the toll route, and the verified school campus. Get those seven things for two or three specific homes and the answer will be obvious.
