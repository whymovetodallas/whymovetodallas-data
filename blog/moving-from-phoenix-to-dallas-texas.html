Moving from Phoenix to Dallas: A Family Relocation Guide for 2026

More Arizona families are landing in Dallas-Fort Worth this year, and most of them tell me the same thing once they arrive: the move was worth it, but a few things surprised them. If you are weighing a move from Phoenix to Dallas, this guide gives you the honest numbers and the real trade offs, from cost of living and home prices to the heat you already know and the humidity you do not, plus which DFW suburbs actually fit families coming from the Valley.

The honest headline

Phoenix and Dallas are more alike than most people expect. Both are big, fast growing Sun Belt metros with no income tax at the city level, strong job markets, and plenty of new construction. Phoenix is actually a little cheaper day to day, around 6 percent lower on overall cost of living. But two things tend to push families toward DFW: Dallas home prices have been more attainable than Phoenix's for the kind of family home buyers want, and Texas charges zero state income tax, while Arizona still does. The move is rarely about saving on groceries. It is about housing, taxes, and schools.

Read that home price line carefully, because it is the one that moves families. While Phoenix's overall cost of living is slightly lower, its home prices have climbed well past Dallas's for comparable properties. For a family buying rather than renting, DFW often delivers more house for the money, especially once you head into the northern suburbs. Rent is close to a wash, and Phoenix's higher median income reflects its tech and semiconductor wage base. The clean win that nobody can argue with is the tax line: Texas has no state income tax, and on a six figure household income that difference is real money every year.

One thing not to expect a discount on is property tax. Texas property taxes are high, noticeably higher than Arizona's, because Texas funds itself through property rather than income. Budget for it. Many of my Arizona clients find the no income tax savings more than offsets it, but you should run your own numbers on a specific home.

The heat trade off (it is real, just different)

You are leaving the land of 110 degree summers, so let me be straight with you. Dallas summers are hot too, but they are humid hot rather than dry hot. Phoenix days above 110 are projected to keep climbing in the years ahead, which is one reason some Valley families are looking at cooler, more affordable markets in the first place. DFW trades that extreme dry heat for muggy summers, real spring storms (hail and the occasional tornado watch), and short, mild winters. Most Arizona transplants tell me they adjust within a season. The biggest practical change is that lawns are green here without a fight, and you will use the word humidity a lot in July.

Jobs and the remote worker angle

Dallas-Fort Worth has one of the deepest, most diversified job markets in the country, anchored by corporate headquarters and finance. Major employers include Toyota North America, JPMorgan Chase, Charles Schwab, State Farm, and American Airlines, many of them clustered in the northern suburbs. If you are transferring with a company or job hunting after the move, that density is a real advantage. And if you are a remote worker simply chasing more home and lower taxes, DFW is a natural upgrade from Phoenix without giving up big city amenities or a major airport.

Where Arizona families actually land in DFW

The Phoenix families I help tend to gravitate to the northern suburbs, where the newer construction, master planned communities, and top rated schools remind them of the better parts of the East Valley, just greener. A few that come up again and again:

  • Frisco and Prosper for new construction, A rated schools, and a polished, planned feel.
  • Argyle and Northlake for a little more land and a small town pace with strong schools.
  • Flower Mound and Highland Village for established neighborhoods near Grapevine Lake.
  • McKinney and Allen for value and a strong sense of community.

Start with my guides to the best DFW suburbs for families and the best school districts for DFW families, then narrow by your commute. As always, the golden rule applies: verify the exact school assignment for any address before you commit, because district lines do not follow city lines.

Is moving from Phoenix to Dallas worth it?

For most families I work with, yes, especially if you are buying a home and earning a solid income, because the no state income tax advantage plus more attainable home prices outweigh the property tax bump and the humidity adjustment. If you rent and your budget is tight, the math is closer, since Phoenix runs slightly cheaper overall. The deciding factors are almost always housing, taxes, and schools, not the cost of a gallon of milk.

Frequently asked questions

Is Dallas cheaper than Phoenix?

On overall cost of living, Phoenix is about 6 percent cheaper. But Dallas home prices are typically lower than Phoenix's for comparable family homes, and Texas has no state income tax, which often makes Dallas the better financial move for families who are buying and earning a solid income.

Does Texas have lower taxes than Arizona?

Texas has no state income tax, while Arizona does, so high earners often save meaningfully by moving. The catch is that Texas property taxes are higher than Arizona's. For many families the income tax savings more than offset the property tax increase, but you should run the numbers on a specific home.

Is Dallas as hot as Phoenix?

Dallas summers are hot but humid rather than dry, and it rarely reaches Phoenix's 110 plus extremes. In exchange you get spring storms and short, mild winters. Most Arizona transplants adjust within one summer.

What are the best DFW suburbs for families moving from Phoenix?

Arizona families often choose Frisco, Prosper, Argyle, Northlake, Flower Mound, Highland Village, McKinney, and Allen for their newer construction, master planned communities, and A rated schools. Choose by commute and verify the school zoning before buying.
